    Section 2.10 Surface Integrals: Flux Through a Surface

    How much fluid crosses a surface per unit time? For a flat surface in a constant velocity field the answer is a single dot product; for a curved surface in a varying field it is a surface integral. In this section we build the surface integral in three steps: we learn how to parametrize a surface, how to use the parametrization to find a normal vector, and how to combine the two to compute the flux of a vector field.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.10.1 Flux Through a Flat Surface

    Suppose water flows with constant velocity \(\mathbf{v}_0\) through a flat surface \(S\) of area \(A\) that is perpendicular to the flow. How much water passes through \(S\) in one second, and in what units? Now tilt the surface, so that the flow meets it at an angle \(\theta\) away from the normal direction, as in Figure 2.10.1: only the component of the velocity perpendicular to the surface pushes water through it. Write the flow rate, and then rewrite it as a dot product of \(\mathbf{v}_0\) with a normal vector \(\mathbf{n} = \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,A\) whose length is the area of \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Our goal is to generalize the above scenario in two respects: (I) we generalize the surface \(S\) (not just a plane), and (II) we generalize the vector field (not just a constant). To achieve this goal, we will need to learn: (I) how to parametrize a surface \(S\text{;}\) (II) how to use the parametrization of \(S\) to find the normal vector \(\mathbf{n}\text{;}\) and (III) how to use (I) and (II) to compute the flux of a vector field. Watch Figure 2.10.2 for the flat-plate picture.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.10.2 Parametric Surfaces

    Recall how we parametrize curves in the \(xy\)-plane: the parabola \(y = x^2\) is traced by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle t, t^2 \rangle\) for \(t \in \R\text{;}\) only one parameter is needed. A surface is two-dimensional, so its parametrization needs two parameters.

    Subsection 2.10.3 The Normal Vector to a Surface

    Consider the unit sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1\) with its spherical parametrization
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \mathbf{r}(\theta,\phi) = \langle \sin\phi\cos\theta,\, \sin\phi\sin\theta,\, \cos\phi \rangle.\tag{2.10.1} \end{equation}
    Our goal is to find the normal \(\mathbf{n}\) to the sphere at every point on its surface. Holding one parameter fixed and varying the other produces two tangent vectors,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}_\phi = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial \phi} = \lim_{\Delta\phi \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r}(\theta, \phi + \Delta\phi) - \mathbf{r}(\theta,\phi)}{\Delta\phi}, \qquad \mathbf{r}_\theta = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial \theta} = \lim_{\Delta\theta \to 0} \frac{\mathbf{r}(\theta + \Delta\theta, \phi) - \mathbf{r}(\theta,\phi)}{\Delta\theta}, \end{equation*}
    so that the two edges of a small coordinate patch on the sphere are approximately \(\mathbf{r}_\phi\, \Delta\phi\) and \(\mathbf{r}_\theta\, \Delta\theta\text{,}\) as in Figure 2.10.5. Their cross product is perpendicular to the patch, so it gives a normal vector, and its length gives the area of the patch:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \mathbf{n} \approx (\mathbf{r}_\phi \times \mathbf{r}_\theta)\, \Delta\phi\, \Delta\theta, \qquad \Delta\sigma \approx \lVert \mathbf{r}_\phi \times \mathbf{r}_\theta \rVert\, \Delta\phi\, \Delta\theta.\tag{2.10.2} \end{equation}
    Carry out this computation for the sphere: differentiate (2.10.1) to get \(\mathbf{r}_\phi\) and \(\mathbf{r}_\theta\text{,}\) take their cross product, and simplify it using \(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1\text{;}\) you should be able to write the result as a multiple of \(\langle x, y, z\rangle\text{.}\) Then normalize, using the fact that \(x^2+y^2+z^2 = 1\) on the sphere, and say in words which way the unit normal points. Compare with Figure 2.10.6.
    🔗

    The Normal to the Unit Sphere.

    For the parametrization (2.10.1) of the unit sphere,
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{r}_\phi \times \mathbf{r}_\theta = \sin\phi\, \langle \sin\phi\cos\theta,\, \sin\phi\sin\theta,\, \cos\phi \rangle = \sin\phi\, \langle x, y, z \rangle,\tag{2.10.3} \end{gather}
    and the unit normal is
    \begin{gather} \hat{\mathbf{n}} = \langle x, y, z \rangle = \langle \sin\phi\cos\theta,\, \sin\phi\sin\theta,\, \cos\phi \rangle,\tag{2.10.4} \end{gather}
    the position vector of the point itself: it points radially outward.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.10.4 Oriented Surfaces

    For a closed surface, that is, a surface that is the boundary of a solid region \(E\text{,}\) the convention is that the positive orientation is the one for which the normal vectors point outward from \(E\text{,}\) and inward-pointing normals give the negative orientation.

    Subsection 2.10.5 The Surface Integral of a Vector Field

    The following definition should be compared to the definition of the line integral of a vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) along a curve \(C\text{,}\) i.e. \(\int_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf{T}\) was defined as the unit tangent vector to the curve \(C\text{.}\) For a surface, the unit tangent is replaced by the unit normal.
    🔗

    Definition 2.10.9.

    If \(\mathbf{F}\) is a continuous vector field defined on an oriented surface \(S\) with unit normal vector \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{,}\) then the surface integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) over \(S\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma.\tag{2.10.5} \end{equation}
    This integral is also called the flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.10.10.

    If the surface \(S\) is parametrized with respect to \(u\) and \(v\text{,}\) we can write the unit normal vector as \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \dfrac{\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v} {\lVert \mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v \rVert}\text{,}\) which can be combined with the fact that \(d\sigma = \lVert \mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v \rVert\, du\, dv\) (from (2.10.2)) to write the surface integral as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma = \iint_R \mathbf{F} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v)\, du\, dv,\tag{2.10.6} \end{equation}
    where \(R\) is the region of the parameters in the \(uv\)-plane. The normalizing length cancels against the area factor, which is what makes this formula so computable.
    🔗
    🔗
    For instance, if the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) is the velocity field of a fluid, then the surface integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) over the surface \(S\) is the net rate at which fluid is crossing \(S\) in the chosen positive direction. As another example, if the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) represents the electric field due to a charged particle such as an electron, then the surface integral of \(\mathbf{F}\) over the surface \(S\) corresponds to the flux of the electric field through the surface \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 2.10.13. Flux Across a Closed Surface.

    Evaluate the surface integral \(\displaystyle \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\text{,}\) where \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = y\,\mathbf{i} + x\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k}\) and the surface \(S\) is the boundary of the solid region \(E\) enclosed by the paraboloid \(z = 1 - x^2 - y^2\) and the plane \(z = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (The closed surface has two pieces, shown in Figure 2.10.15: the paraboloid cap \(S_1\) and the unit disk \(S_2\) in the plane \(z = 0\text{.}\) Which way must the outward normal point on each?
