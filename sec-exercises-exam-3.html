    1. (4 points).

    Consider the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_{3x}^{3x+6} \left(\frac{y-3x}{3} + \frac{z}{2}\right) dy\,dx\,dz . \end{equation*}
    Rewrite (do not evaluate) this integral in such a way that the limits of integration are all constants (as opposed to functions).
    🔗

    Solution.

    The limits on \(x\) and \(z\) are already constants; only the limits on \(y\text{,}\) \(3x \le y \le 3x + 6\text{,}\) depend on \(x\text{.}\) They say \(0 \le y - 3x \le 6\text{:}\) in the \(xy\)-plane the region is the parallelogram of Figure 4.14.1. The integrand contains the same combination \(\frac{y-3x}{3}\text{,}\) together with \(\frac{z}{2}\text{,}\) so let
    \begin{equation*} u = \frac{y-3x}{3}, \quad v = x, \quad w = \frac{z}{2} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    New limits. \(0 \le y - 3x \le 6\) gives \(0 \le u \le 2\text{;}\) \(0 \le x \le 1\) gives \(0 \le v \le 1\text{;}\) \(0 \le z \le 2\) gives \(0 \le w \le 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Jacobian. Solving for the old variables, \(x = v\text{,}\) \(y = 3u + 3v\text{,}\) \(z = 2w\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} 0 \amp 1 \amp 0\\ 3 \amp 3 \amp 0\\ 0 \amp 0 \amp 2 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = 2\,(0 \cdot 3 - 1 \cdot 3) = -6, \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    and \(dy\,dx\,dz = |-6|\,du\,dv\,dw = 6\,du\,dv\,dw\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The integral. The integrand becomes \(u + w\text{,}\) so
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_{3x}^{3x+6}\\ \amp \qquad \left(\frac{y-3x}{3} + \frac{z}{2}\right) dy\,dx\,dz\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2 6\,(u + w)\,du\,dv\,dw . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Other substitutions are equally correct. For example \(u = y - 3x\text{,}\) \(v = x\text{,}\) \(w = z\) has Jacobian \(1\) and gives
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^2\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^6 \left(\frac{u}{3} + \frac{w}{2}\right) du\,dv\,dw . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Value (not required):
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2 6\,(u + w)\,du\,dv\,dw\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^1 (12 + 12w)\,dw = 18 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    2. (4 points).

    A two-dimensional object has the shape of the petal of the rose \(r = \sin(2\theta)\) that lies in the first quadrant, and its density is \(\delta(r,\theta) = 2 - r\text{.}\) Set up (do not evaluate) all the integrals required to compute the center of mass of this object.
    🔗

    Solution.

    The region. On \(0 \le \theta \le \pi/2\) we have \(\sin(2\theta) \ge 0\text{,}\) with \(r = 0\) at \(\theta = 0\) and at \(\theta = \pi/2\text{:}\) as \(\theta\) runs from \(0\) to \(\pi/2\) the curve leaves the origin and returns to it, tracing the petal in the first quadrant (Figure 4.14.2). So the object is
    \begin{gather*} R:\quad 0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2},\\ 0 \le r \le \sin(2\theta) . \end{gather*}
    🔗
    The integrals. In polar coordinates \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\text{,}\) \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\text{.}\) The mass and the two moments are
    \begin{align*} M \amp = \iint_R \delta\,dA\\ \amp = \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\sin(2\theta)} (2 - r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta,\\ M_y \amp = \iint_R x\,\delta\,dA\\ \amp = \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\sin(2\theta)}\\ \amp \qquad (r\cos\theta)\,(2 - r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta,\\ M_x \amp = \iint_R y\,\delta\,dA\\ \amp = \int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\sin(2\theta)}\\ \amp \qquad (r\sin\theta)\,(2 - r)\,r\,dr\,d\theta, \end{align*}
    and the center of mass is
    \begin{equation*} \bar x = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar y = \frac{M_x}{M} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Values (not required): \(M = \frac{\pi}{4} - \frac29\) and \(M_x = M_y = \frac{64}{315}\text{,}\) so \(\bar x = \bar y = \dfrac{64/315}{\pi/4 - 2/9} \approx 0.36\text{.}\) The two coordinates agree because the petal and the density are symmetric about the line \(y = x\text{.}\)

    3. (4 points).

    Let \(C\) be the closed curve parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t - t^3,\ t^2 \right\rangle\) on \(-1 \le t \le 1\text{,}\) enclosing the region \(R\) (Figure 4.14.3).
    🔗

    (a)

    Compute the work done by the force \(\mathbf{F} = \left\langle -y,\ 0 \right\rangle\) along the closed curve \(C\text{.}\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    The velocity is \(\mathbf{r}'(t) = \left\langle 1 - 3t^2,\ 2t \right\rangle\text{,}\) and on the curve \(y = t^2\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) = \left\langle -t^2,\ 0 \right\rangle\text{.}\) The work is
    \begin{align*} W \amp = \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp = \int_{-1}^{1} \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt\\ \amp = \int_{-1}^{1} \langle -t^2, 0 \rangle \cdot \langle 1 - 3t^2, 2t \rangle\,dt\\ \amp = \int_{-1}^{1} \left(3t^4 - t^2\right) dt\\ \amp = \left[\frac{3t^5}{5} - \frac{t^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{1}\\ \amp = \frac65 - \frac23 = \frac{8}{15} . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Use Green’s theorem with the field \(\mathbf{F} = \left\langle -y,\ 0 \right\rangle\) of part (a) to show that the area of \(R\) is equal to the line integral \(\displaystyle\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{.}\) Then find the area of \(R\text{.}\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Write \(\mathbf{F} = \langle M, N \rangle\) with \(M = -y\) and \(N = 0\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} = 0 - (-1) = 1 . \end{equation*}
    The curve \(C\) is a simple closed curve traversed counterclockwise (Figure 4.14.3), and \(M\text{,}\) \(N\) have continuous partial derivatives everywhere, so Green’s theorem applies:
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right) dA\\ \amp \quad = \iint_R 1\,dA = \operatorname{Area}(R) . \end{align*}
    By part (a), \(\operatorname{Area}(R) = \frac{8}{15}\text{.}\)

    🔗

    4. (4 points).

    Let \(S\) be the upper hemisphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\text{,}\) \(z \ge 0\text{,}\) oriented by its upward unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{.}\) Compute the flux of the vector field
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = (x + 2y)\,\mathbf{i} + (y - 2x)\,\mathbf{j} + z\,\mathbf{k} \end{equation*}
    through \(S\text{,}\) that is, \(\displaystyle\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,d\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    The normal. \(S\) is part of the sphere of radius \(2\) centered at the origin. At a point \((x,y,z)\) of a sphere centered at the origin, the outward unit normal is the position vector divided by its length (Figure 4.14.4):
    \begin{equation*} \hat{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{\langle x,\ y,\ z \rangle}{2} . \end{equation*}
    Its \(\mathbf{k}\)-component \(z/2\) is \(\ge 0\) on \(S\text{,}\) so this is the upward normal. No parametrization is needed.
    🔗
    The integrand. On \(S\text{,}\)
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\\ \amp \quad = \frac{(x + 2y)\,x + (y - 2x)\,y + z \cdot z}{2}\\ \amp \quad = \frac{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}{2} = \frac42 = 2, \end{align*}
    since the terms \(2xy\) and \(-2xy\) cancel and \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\) on the sphere.
    🔗
    The flux. The integrand is constant on \(S\text{,}\) so the flux is that constant times the area of \(S\text{,}\) half the area \(4\pi \cdot 2^2\) of the sphere:
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,d\sigma = \iint_S 2\,d\sigma\\ \amp \quad = 2 \cdot \operatorname{Area}(S) = 2 \cdot 8\pi = 16\pi . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5. (4 points).

    Let \(C\) be the curve in which the plane \(x + z = 3\) cuts the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 4\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise when seen from above. Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \qquad\text{for}\qquad \mathbf{F} = \langle z,\ 0,\ x \rangle . \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Solution.

    The quickest route is Stokes’ theorem.
    🔗
    The curl.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{aligned} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k}\\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z}\\ z \amp 0 \amp x \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = (0 - 0)\,\mathbf{i} - (1 - 1)\,\mathbf{j}\\ \amp \qquad + (0 - 0)\,\mathbf{k} = \mathbf{0} . \end{aligned} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The surface. Let \(S\) be the part of the plane \(x + z = 3\) inside the cylinder (Figure 4.14.5), oriented by its upward unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}} = \dfrac{\langle 1,\ 0,\ 1 \rangle}{\sqrt2}\text{;}\) its boundary is \(C\text{,}\) and the upward normal matches the counterclockwise direction on \(C\) by the right-hand rule.
    🔗
    The integral. By Stokes’ theorem,
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \iint_S (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,d\sigma\\ \amp \quad = \iint_S \mathbf{0} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\,d\sigma = 0 . \end{align*}
    Since the curl vanishes identically, neither the normal nor \(d\sigma\) has to be computed, and any surface with boundary \(C\) gives the same answer.
    🔗
    Other methods. The problem does not prescribe a method, and two others are equally correct. (i) \(\mathbf{F} = \langle z, 0, x \rangle = \nabla(xz)\) is conservative, so its line integral around any closed curve is \(0\text{.}\) (ii) Directly: with \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle 2\cos t,\ 2\sin t,\ 3 - 2\cos t \rangle\text{,}\) \(0 \le t \le 2\pi\text{,}\) we get \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = -6\sin t + 8\sin t\cos t\text{,}\) whose integral over \([0, 2\pi]\) is \(0\text{.}\)

    6. (bonus, 6 points).

    Without calculating the integrals, decide whether each of the following integrals is positive, negative, or zero. Let \(S\) be the solid above \(z = -\sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}\) and below \(z = -\sqrt{3x^2 + 3y^2}\text{.}\) Remember to justify your responses.
    🔗

    (a)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_S \left(z^2 - 3x^2 - 3y^2\right) dV\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First locate \(S\text{.}\) The surface \(z = -\sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2}\) is the lower half of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 4\text{,}\) and \(z = -\sqrt{3x^2 + 3y^2}\) is the lower half of the cone \(z^2 = 3\left(x^2 + y^2\right)\text{.}\) Writing \(r^2 = x^2 + y^2\text{,}\) they meet where \(4 - r^2 = 3r^2\text{,}\) that is, on the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\) at height \(z = -\sqrt3\text{,}\) and inside that circle the cone is on top. So
    \begin{align*} \amp S:\quad x^2 + y^2 \le 1,\\ \amp -\sqrt{4 - x^2 - y^2} \le z\\ \amp \qquad \le -\sqrt{3x^2 + 3y^2} \end{align*}
    (Figure 4.14.6): an upside-down ice-cream cone.
    🔗
    Positive. On \(S\) we have \(z \le -\sqrt{3x^2 + 3y^2} \le 0\text{,}\) so \(|z| \ge \sqrt{3x^2 + 3y^2}\) and therefore \(z^2 \ge 3x^2 + 3y^2\text{.}\) The integrand \(z^2 - 3x^2 - 3y^2\) is \(\ge 0\) throughout \(S\text{,}\) and it is \(0\) only on the cone, a surface of zero volume. An integrand that is positive throughout the solid except on a surface of zero volume has a positive integral. (Its value is \(\frac{64\pi}{15}\left(3\sqrt3 - 5\right) \approx 2.63\text{,}\) but no calculation is expected.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_S \left(y^4 - y^2\right) dV\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Negative. Factor: \(y^4 - y^2 = y^2\left(y^2 - 1\right)\text{.}\) On \(S\text{,}\) described in the solution of part (a), we have \(y^2 \le x^2 + y^2 \le 1\text{,}\) so \(y^2 \ge 0\) and \(y^2 - 1 \le 0\text{:}\) the integrand is \(\le 0\) throughout \(S\text{,}\) and it is \(0\) only where \(y = 0\) or \(y = \pm 1\text{,}\) a set of zero volume. So the integral is negative. (Its value is \(\frac{\pi}{105}\left(320 - 189\sqrt3\right) \approx -0.22\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    \(\displaystyle\iiint_S x\,z^2\,dV\) (2 points)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Zero. \(S\text{,}\) described in the solution of part (a), is symmetric about the plane \(x = 0\text{:}\) its bounding surfaces involve \(x\) only through \(x^2\text{,}\) so the reflection \(x \mapsto -x\) maps \(S\) onto itself. The integrand is odd under that reflection, \((-x)\,z^2 = -x\,z^2\text{:}\) the half \(x \ge 0\) of \(S\) and the half \(x \le 0\) contribute opposite amounts, and the integral is \(0\text{.}\)
