    Section 2.8 Applications: Mass and Center of Mass

    Multiple integrals let us compute physical quantities of solid objects and flat plates whose density varies from point to point. In this section we use double and triple integrals to compute the total mass of an object and to locate its center of mass—the point at which the object would balance perfectly.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.1 Finding the Center of Mass of a 3D Object

    Suppose a solid object occupies a region \(D\) in space and its density (mass per unit volume) at each point is given by a continuous function \(\delta(x,y,z)\text{.}\) To measure the total mass, we partition \(D\) into small boxes, as in Figure 2.8.2. If the \(k\)-th box contains the point \((x_k, y_k, z_k)\) and has volume \(\Delta V_k\text{,}\) write down its approximate mass, sum over the boxes, and let the size of the boxes shrink to zero.
    🔗

    Mass of a Solid.

    \begin{equation} M = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \delta(x_k, y_k, z_k)\, \Delta V_k = \iiint_D \delta(x,y,z)\, dV.\tag{2.8.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Watch the construction unfold in Figure 2.8.1.
    🔗
    The same idea locates the balance point of the object. Each small box contributes a moment about each coordinate plane—its mass times its (signed) distance from that plane. Adding these contributions and passing to the limit gives the first moments
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M_{yz} = \iiint_D x\, \delta\, dV, \qquad M_{xz} = \iiint_D y\, \delta\, dV, \qquad M_{xy} = \iiint_D z\, \delta\, dV.\tag{2.8.2} \end{equation}
    The center of mass \((\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{z})\) is the point whose coordinates are the moments divided by the total mass:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_{yz}}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_{xz}}{M}, \qquad \bar{z} = \frac{M_{xy}}{M}.\tag{2.8.3} \end{equation}

    Example 2.8.3. Center of Mass of a Solid Bounded by a Paraboloid.

    Find the center of mass of a solid of constant density \(\delta\text{,}\) which is bounded by the disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 4\) (in the plane \(z = 0\)) and the paraboloid \(z = 4 - x^2 - y^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (The solid is shown in Figure 2.8.5. Use its symmetry to dispose of two of the three coordinates at once. For the third, work in cylindrical coordinates with (2.6.2): describe the region, compute \(M\) by (2.8.1) and the needed moment by (2.8.2), and divide as in (2.8.3). What happens to \(\delta\text{?}\) Compare with Figure 2.8.4.)

    Subsection 2.8.2 Centroids

    Definition 2.8.6.

    When the density of a solid object (as in the previous example) or plate (as in the next example) is constant, the center of mass is called the centroid of the object.
    🔗
    🔗
    As we saw in Example 2.8.3, a constant density \(\delta\) cancels in the quotients of (2.8.3), so the centroid depends only on the shape of the object. For a flat plate occupying a region \(R\) in the plane with density \(\delta(x,y)\) (mass per unit area), the same reasoning that gave (2.8.1) and (2.8.2)—with double integrals in place of triple integrals—gives the mass and first moments
    🔗
    \begin{equation} M = \iint_R \delta\, dA, \qquad M_y = \iint_R x\, \delta\, dA, \qquad M_x = \iint_R y\, \delta\, dA,\tag{2.8.4} \end{equation}
    where \(M_y\) is the moment about the \(y\)-axis and \(M_x\) the moment about the \(x\)-axis, and the center of mass is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M}.\tag{2.8.5} \end{equation}

    Example 2.8.7. Centroid of the Region Between Two Curves.

    Find the centroid of the region between \(y = \sqrt{x}\) and \(y = x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Find where the curves meet, and describe the region, shown in Figure 2.8.9. Since only the centroid is wanted, what may you take \(\delta\) to be, and what does \(M\) then measure? Compute \(M\text{,}\) \(M_y\) and \(M_x\) from (2.8.4), divide as in (2.8.5), and explain from the picture why \(\bar x = \bar y\) had to happen. Compare with Figure 2.8.8.)

    Example 2.8.10. Centroid of a Cardioid.

    Find the centroid of the object that has the shape \(r = 1 + \cos(\theta)\) and density \(\delta = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (The cardioid is shown in Figure 2.8.12. Use its symmetry to dispose of one coordinate. Work in polar coordinates with \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\) from (2.4.1) and \(x = r\cos\theta\text{:}\) compute \(M\) and \(M_y\) from (2.8.4). In the \(\theta\)-integrals, the odd powers of cosine integrate to zero over a full period, \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi\text{,}\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^4\theta\,d\theta = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\text{.}\) Then divide as in (2.8.5); what happens to \(\delta = 2\text{?}\) Compare with Figure 2.8.11.)
