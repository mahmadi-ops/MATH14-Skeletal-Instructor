    Section 2.8 Applications: Mass and Center of Mass

    Multiple integrals let us compute physical quantities of solid objects and flat plates whose density varies from point to point. In this section we use double and triple integrals to compute the total mass of an object and to locate its center of mass—the point at which the object would balance perfectly. Before we study continuous objects, we review what the center of mass of a system of point masses is and why it deserves its name: first for two masses, then for any number of them. A flat plate and a solid are then handled in the same way, by cutting them into many small pieces and treating each piece as a point mass.
    🔗
    Starred material is optional. In this section that is Subsection 2.8.4, on point masses in space and solid objects.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.1 The Center of Mass of Two Point Masses

    Consider two masses \(m_1\) and \(m_2\) located at the points \(\mathbf{r}_1 = (x_1, y_1)\) and \(\mathbf{r}_2 = (x_2, y_2)\text{,}\) as in Figure 2.8.1, and let \(\mathbf{F}_1\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2\) be the net forces acting on them. By Newton’s second law, each force is the mass times the acceleration, \(\mathbf{F}_1 = m_1\, \dfrac{d^2\mathbf{r}_1}{dt^2}\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2 = m_2\, \dfrac{d^2\mathbf{r}_2}{dt^2}\text{.}\) Write the total force \(\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2\) on the system of the two masses, then multiply and divide by the total mass \(M = m_1 + m_2\) to bring it to the familiar form \(\mathbf{F} = M\mathbf{a}\) of Newton’s second law.
    🔗

    Center of Mass of Two Point Masses.

    The total force is \(\mathbf{F} = M\, \dfrac{d^2\bar{\mathbf{r}}}{dt^2}\text{,}\) where the center of mass of the system is the point
    \begin{align} \bar{\mathbf{r}} \amp = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{2} m_i\, \mathbf{r}_i\notag\\ \amp = \left( \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2},\ \frac{m_1 y_1 + m_2 y_2}{m_1 + m_2} \right).\tag{2.8.1} \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗
    Thus the total force equals the total mass times the acceleration of a virtual mass \(M\) located at \(\bar{\mathbf{r}}\text{:}\) as far as the net force is concerned, the pair of masses moves like a single particle of mass \(M\) sitting at the center of mass. The formula is a weighted average of the two positions, with the masses as the weights. If \(m_1 = m_2\text{,}\) the center of mass is the midpoint of the segment joining the two masses; otherwise it lies on that segment, closer to the heavier mass.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.2 The Center of Mass of \(n\) Point Masses and the First Moments

    Nothing in the computation above depended on there being only two masses. For a system of \(n\) point masses \(m_1, \dots, m_n\) at the points \(\mathbf{r}_1, \dots, \mathbf{r}_n\text{,}\) adding Newton’s second law for each mass and multiplying and dividing by the total mass \(M = m_1 + \cdots + m_n\) gives, exactly as before, \(\mathbf{F} = M\, \dfrac{d^2\bar{\mathbf{r}}}{dt^2}\) with
    🔗
    \begin{align} \bar{\mathbf{r}} \amp = \frac{1}{M} \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, \mathbf{r}_i,\tag{2.8.2}\\ M \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i .\notag \end{align}
    The sums in the coordinates of \(\bar{\mathbf{r}}\) have names of their own. For masses in the plane, with \(\mathbf{r}_i = (x_i, y_i)\text{,}\) the first moments of the system are
    🔗
    \begin{align} M_y \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, x_i,\tag{2.8.3}\\ M_x \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, y_i .\notag \end{align}
    \(M_y\) is the moment about the \(y\)-axis: each mass is multiplied by its signed distance \(x_i\) from the \(y\)-axis, so \(M_y\) measures the tendency of the system to tip about that axis (masses to the right of the axis, with \(x_i \gt 0\text{,}\) count positively, and masses to its left count negatively). Likewise \(M_x\) is the moment about the \(x\)-axis, computed with the signed distances \(y_i\) from the \(x\)-axis. In this notation, (2.8.2) says that the center of mass \(\bar{\mathbf{r}} = (\bar{x}, \bar{y})\) has the coordinates
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M}.\tag{2.8.4} \end{equation}
    Every formula for a flat plate in the next subsection is one of these, with the sum over point masses replaced by an integral over the plate. Masses in space, and solid objects, are treated in Subsection 2.8.4.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.8.3 Finding the Center of Mass of a 2D Object

    Now let the mass be spread continuously over a flat plate that occupies a region \(R\) in the plane, with density \(\delta(x,y)\) (mass per unit area) at the point \((x,y)\text{.}\) To bring the formulas of the previous subsection to bear, we cut the plate into small pieces and treat each piece as a point mass. Partition the plane into small rectangles by the lines \(x = x_0, x_1, \dots, x_n\) and \(y = y_0, y_1, \dots, y_m\text{,}\) as in Figure 2.8.2. The rectangle in column \(i\) and row \(j\) has width \(\Delta x_i\text{,}\) height \(\Delta y_j\text{,}\) and area \(\Delta A_{ij} = \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\text{;}\) if it meets \(R\text{,}\) we pick a point \((x_i, y_j)\) in it. Write down the approximate mass \(\Delta m_{ij}\) of this small piece, treat the pieces as a system of point masses, and use (2.8.2) and (2.8.3) to write the mass and the first moments of the plate as double sums; then let the rectangles shrink, \(m, n \to \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Mass of a Plate.

    \begin{align} \amp M = \lim_{m,n \to \infty} \sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\notag\\ \amp \quad \delta(x_i, y_j)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\notag\\ \amp = \iint_R \delta(x,y)\, dA.\tag{2.8.5} \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗
    The first moments become double integrals in the same way. So the mass and the first moments of the plate are
    🔗
    \begin{align} M \amp = \iint_R \delta\, dA,\notag\\ M_y \amp = \iint_R x\, \delta\, dA,\tag{2.8.6}\\ M_x \amp = \iint_R y\, \delta\, dA,\notag \end{align}
    where, as before, \(M_y\) is the moment about the \(y\)-axis and \(M_x\) the moment about the \(x\)-axis, and the center of mass is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \bar{x} = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar{y} = \frac{M_x}{M}.\tag{2.8.7} \end{equation}

    Definition 2.8.3.

    When the density of a plate or of a solid object is constant, the center of mass is called the centroid of the object.
    🔗
    🔗
    A constant density \(\delta\) cancels in the quotients of (2.8.7), so the centroid depends only on the shape of the object: to find a centroid we may as well take \(\delta = 1\text{,}\) and then the mass \(M\) is simply the area of the plate.

    Subsection 2.8.4 Finding the Center of Mass of a 3D Object*

    Everything so far took place in the plane. For point masses in space, with \(\mathbf{r}_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i)\) in (2.8.2), the distances are measured from the coordinate planes instead of the coordinate axes. The first moments about the coordinate planes are
    🔗
    \begin{align} M_{yz} \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, x_i,\notag\\ M_{xz} \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, y_i,\tag{2.8.8}\\ M_{xy} \amp = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i\, z_i\notag \end{align}
    (\(M_{yz}\) uses the signed distance \(x_i\) from the \(yz\)-plane, and so on), and the center of mass \(\bar{\mathbf{r}} = (\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{z})\) is
    🔗
    \begin{align} \bar{x} \amp = \frac{M_{yz}}{M},\notag\\ \bar{y} \amp = \frac{M_{xz}}{M},\tag{2.8.9}\\ \bar{z} \amp = \frac{M_{xy}}{M}.\notag \end{align}
    Now let a solid object occupy a region \(D\) in space, with density \(\delta(x,y,z)\) (mass per unit volume) at the point \((x,y,z)\text{.}\) The plan is the same as for a plate, one dimension up: we cut the solid into small boxes and treat each box as a point mass. Partition space into boxes by the planes \(x = x_0, \dots, x_n\text{,}\) \(y = y_0, \dots, y_m\text{,}\) and \(z = z_0, \dots, z_p\text{.}\) The box in position \(i\) along the \(x\)-direction, \(j\) along the \(y\)-direction, and \(k\) along the \(z\)-direction has the dimensions \(\Delta x_i\text{,}\) \(\Delta y_j\text{,}\) \(\Delta z_k\) and the volume \(\Delta V_{ijk} = \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\, \Delta z_k\text{;}\) if it meets \(D\text{,}\) we pick a point \((x_i, y_j, z_k)\) in it, as in Figure 2.8.11. Write down the approximate mass \(\Delta m_{ijk}\) of this small box, treat the boxes as a system of point masses, and use (2.8.2) and (2.8.8) to write the mass and the first moments of the solid as triple sums; then let the boxes shrink, \(n, m, p \to \infty\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Mass of a Solid.

    \begin{align} \amp M = \lim_{n,m,p \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{p}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\notag\\ \amp \quad \delta(x_i, y_j, z_k)\, \Delta x_i\, \Delta y_j\, \Delta z_k\notag\\ \amp = \iiint_D \delta(x,y,z)\, dV.\tag{2.8.10} \end{align}
    🔗
    🔗
    Watch the construction unfold in Figure 2.8.10.
    🔗
    In the same way, the first moments become the triple integrals
    🔗
    \begin{align} M_{yz} \amp = \iiint_D x\, \delta\, dV,\notag\\ M_{xz} \amp = \iiint_D y\, \delta\, dV,\tag{2.8.11}\\ M_{xy} \amp = \iiint_D z\, \delta\, dV,\notag \end{align}
    the moments about the \(yz\)-, \(xz\)-, and \(xy\)-planes, and the center of mass \((\bar{x}, \bar{y}, \bar{z})\) of the solid is the point whose coordinates are the moments divided by the total mass:
    🔗
    \begin{align} \bar{x} \amp = \frac{M_{yz}}{M},\notag\\ \bar{y} \amp = \frac{M_{xz}}{M},\tag{2.8.12}\\ \bar{z} \amp = \frac{M_{xy}}{M}.\notag \end{align}
