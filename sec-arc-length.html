    Section 1.2 Arc Length of a Curve

    When an object moves along a curve, we often want to know the total distance it travels. In this section we build the arc-length integral, first in the plane and then in space, and we apply it to two motions: a point sweeping out a quarter circle and a paraglider spiralling to the ground. The key idea throughout is simple: the total arc length of a curved path can be approximated as the sum of the arc lengths of \(n\) small pieces.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.1 Arc Length in 2D

    Goal. Compute the arc length \(S\) of a curve using its parametrized form. Imagine that at time \(t=a\) an object starts moving from an initial point \(A\) and at time \(t=b\) it reaches the final point \(B\text{.}\) During this period the object traces out a smooth curve \(C\) in the plane. Throughout this section we illustrate the construction on the closed curve of Figure 1.2.1, a circle traversed once counterclockwise, so that the object returns to where it began and \(A\) and \(B\) are the same point. How can we calculate the distance the object has travelled?
    🔗
    The strategy is to replace the curve by a chain of straight segments whose lengths we already know how to compute, as in Figure 1.2.2.
    🔗
    Now build the arc length from the chords. (Approximate \(S\) by the sum of the chord lengths and let \(n \to \infty\text{.}\))
    🔗

    Results.

    Let a plane curve \(C\) be parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\left\langle x(t),\,y(t)\right\rangle\) for \(a\le t\le b\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation} S = \int_C ds = \int_{t=a}^{t=b}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt,\tag{1.2.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    where
    \begin{equation} \mathbf{v}(t)=\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} =\left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt}\right\rangle, \qquad ds = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt.\tag{1.2.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    In words: arc length is the integral of speed over time. This is the natural generalization of the familiar rule “distance equals speed times time” to motion whose speed and direction change from moment to moment.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.2.3. Length of a quarter circle.

    Using the arc-length formula (1.2.1), calculate the length of a quarter circle of radius one. (We already know the answer: a full unit circle has circumference \(2\pi\text{,}\) so a quarter of it has length \(\dfrac{\pi}{2}\text{.}\) The point of this example is to see the formula reproduce it.)
    🔗
    Solution.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.2 Arc Length in 3D

    The formulas (1.2.1) and (1.2.2) generalize to motion in space with no new ideas: the \(k\)-th chord simply picks up a third leg, so \(\left(\Delta s_k\right)^{2} = \left(\Delta x_k\right)^{2}+\left(\Delta y_k\right)^{2} +\left(\Delta z_k\right)^{2}\) and in the limit \(ds=\sqrt{(dx)^2+(dy)^2+(dz)^2}\text{.}\) For a space curve \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\left\langle x(t),\,y(t),\,z(t)\right\rangle\) we therefore have
    \begin{align} S \amp = \int_C ds = \int_{t=a}^{t=b} \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^{2}}\,dt\tag{1.2.3}\\ \amp = \int_{t=a}^{t=b} \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt,\notag\\ \mathbf{v}(t) \amp = \frac{d\mathbf{r}(t)}{dt} = \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt},\ \frac{dz}{dt}\right\rangle,\notag\\ \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert \amp = \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^{2} + \left(\frac{dz}{dt}\right)^{2}}.\notag \end{align}
    🔗
    As in the plane, arc length is still the integral of speed over time.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.2.3 Activity: Where a Plane Slices a Cone

    Activity 1.2.1. Where a Plane Slices a Cone.

    Let \(S_1\) be the upper half of a circular cone and let \(S_2\) be a slanted plane:
    \begin{equation*} S_1 : \ z^2 = x^2 + y^2, \quad z \ge 0, \qquad\qquad S_2 : \ z = x + 1 . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let \(C = S_1 \cap S_2\) be their curve of intersection, shown in Figure 1.2.10.
    🔗
    (a)
    Show that every point of \(C\) satisfies \(y^2 = 2x + 1\text{,}\) and use this to find a vector parametrization \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle x(t),\,y(t),\,z(t)\right\rangle\) of \(C\text{.}\) Then verify directly that your \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) lies on both surfaces for every \(t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    The curve \(C\) crosses the plane \(x = 0\) at the two points \(P(0,-1,1)\) and \(Q(0,1,1)\text{.}\) Set up the arc-length integral (1.2.3) for the piece of \(C\) joining \(P\) to \(Q\) and evaluate it exactly.
    🔗
    Solution. (Substitute \(u = \sqrt{2}\,t\) to bring the integrand to the form \(\sqrt{1+u^2}\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    (c)
    The point \(V\left(-\frac{1}{2},\, 0,\, \frac{1}{2}\right)\) is the vertex (the lowest point) of \(C\text{.}\) Find the arc-length function \(s(t)\) measured from \(V\text{,}\) and use it to compute the exact length of \(C\) from \(V\) to the point \(\left(\frac{3}{2},\, 2,\, \frac{5}{2}\right)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Which value of \(t\) gives \(V\text{?}\) Start the integral there; the logarithm simplifies, because \(3 + 2\sqrt{2}\) is a perfect square.)
