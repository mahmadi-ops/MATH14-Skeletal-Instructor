    Section 2.1 Double and Iterated Integrals Over Rectangles

    Before we begin discussing the actual topic of today’s lecture, we will briefly remind ourselves of the concept of area under a function and its relation with definite integrals.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.1 Reminder: Area Under \(y = f(x)\) from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\)

    As shown in Figure 2.1.1, to calculate the area under \(y = f(x)\) from \(x = a\) to \(x = b\text{,}\) we divide the interval \((a,b)\) into \(n\) subintervals and then let the number of subintervals (which is the same as the number of rectangles) approach infinity. Write the area as such a limit, and then as the definite integral it defines.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 Double Integrals Over Rectangles

    As we will see, double integrals can be interpreted geometrically as the volume under a surface \(z = f(x,y)\text{.}\) As an example, consider the surface \(z = x^2 + y + 5\) over the region
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R : \{(x,y) : 0 \le x \le 2,\ 0 \le y \le 2\}, \end{equation*}
    drawn in Figure 2.1.2. The solid we are after stands on top of \(R\) and stops at the surface: its floor is the shaded square \(R\) in the \(xy\)-plane, its ceiling is the piece of the surface lying directly above \(R\text{,}\) outlined in dashed black, and the dashed vertical segments above the four corners of \(R\) are its vertical edges. Computing the volume of that solid—the volume on top of the region of integration and below the surface—is the goal of this section. Its ceiling is curved, so no formula from solid geometry gives the answer, and we fall back on the idea of Subsection 2.1.1: estimate the volume with pieces whose volume we do know, then refine.
    🔗
    To estimate that volume, use the point \((1,1)\) in the middle of \(R\) to find the height of a single cuboid standing on \(R\text{,}\) and compute this (very bad) estimate of the desired volume.
    🔗
    We can improve the estimate by splitting the region into more squares. Split \(R\) into four squares, sample \(f\) at the center of each, and add up the four cuboids, as shown in Figure 2.1.4. Similar to finding the area under \(f(x)\text{,}\) if we let the number of splittings go to infinity, our estimation becomes exact, i.e., we find the exact desired volume. The refinement process is animated in Figure 2.1.6.
    🔗
    Write the approximation of the volume in Riemann-sum notation, using \(n\) divisions in \(x\) and \(m\) in \(y\text{,}\) and then the exact volume as a limit. (Each box contributes its base area \(\Delta A_{ij} = \Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j\) times the height of the surface at a sample point.)

    Subsection 2.1.3 Calculating Double Integrals as Iterated Integrals

    Example 2.1.7. Example I.

    Calculate the double integral \(\displaystyle\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^2 \left(x^2 + y + 5\right)\,dx\,dy\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Integrate with respect to \(x\) first, treating \(y\) as a constant and using FTC II; the result is a function of \(y\text{.}\) Then integrate that with respect to \(y\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    In calculating double integrals, the following theorem can be very useful.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.1.8. Fubini’s Theorem (First Form).

    If \(f(x,y)\) is continuous throughout the rectangular region \(R: a \le x \le b,\ c \le y \le d\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f(x,y)\,dx\,dy = \int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f(x,y)\,dy\,dx.\tag{2.1.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    Therefore, if the integrand satisfies the conditions of Theorem 2.1.8, we can change the order of integration as in (2.1.2). Check this on the previous example: integrate over \(y\) first, then over \(x\text{,}\) and compare with the result of Example 2.1.7.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.4 An Iterated Integral Requiring Substitution and Integration by Parts

    Example 2.1.9. Example II.

    Calculate the double integral \(\displaystyle\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^1 \frac{x}{1+xy}\,dx\,dy\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (By Theorem 2.1.8 you may integrate over \(y\) first, with \(x\) held constant; the substitution \(u = 1 + xy\) does it. The result is a function of \(x\) involving a logarithm, which needs integration by parts, \(\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du\text{;}\) in the leftover integral, write the numerator as \((1 + 2x) - 1\text{.}\) The surface is shown in Figure 2.1.10.)
