    Section 2.7 Triple Integrals in Spherical Coordinates

    Today, we will study yet another useful coordinate system in three dimensions, which is known as the spherical coordinate system. It simplifies the evaluation of triple integrals over regions bounded by spheres or cones.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.1 Spherical Coordinates

    The spherical coordinates can be best explained in a figure. Consider the point \(P\) with spherical coordinates \((\rho,\theta,\phi)\) shown in Figure 2.7.1.
    🔗
    1. \(\rho\) is the distance of the point \(P\) from the origin (\(\rho \ge 0\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(\theta\) is the angle the projection of \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) makes with the positive \(x\)-axis. This is the same \(\theta\) as in cylindrical coordinates, (2.6.1) (\(0 \le \theta \le 2\pi\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    3. \(\phi\) is the angle between \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) and the positive \(z\)-axis (\(0 \le \phi \le \pi\)).
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Equations Relating Spherical Coordinates to Cartesian and Cylindrical Coordinates.

    \begin{gather} r = \rho\sin\phi, \qquad x = r\cos\theta = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\tag{2.7.1}\\ z = \rho\cos\phi, \qquad y = r\sin\theta = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\notag\\ \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}.\notag \end{gather}
    🔗
    🔗
    Spheres and cones have particularly simple equations in spherical coordinates. Substitute (2.7.1) into the equation of a sphere of radius \(R\) centered at the origin, \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = R^2\text{,}\) and simplify.
    🔗

    Example 2.7.2. Example I.

    Find the equation of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + (z-1)^2 = 1\) in spherical coordinates.
    🔗
    Solution. (Substitute (2.7.1), expand the square, and use \(\sin^2\phi + \cos^2\phi = 1\text{;}\) a factor of \(\rho\) cancels.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.7.3. Example II.

    What are the equations of the two cones \(z = \sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) and \(z = -\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\) in spherical coordinates?
    🔗
    Solution. (What angle does each cone make with the positive \(z\)-axis? Confirm algebraically by substituting (2.7.1).)
    🔗
    🔗

    Coordinate Conversion Formulas.

    \begin{align*} \text{Cylindrical to rectangular:}\quad \amp x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta, \quad z = z\\ \text{Spherical to rectangular:}\quad \amp x = \rho\sin\phi\cos\theta, \quad y = \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta, \quad z = \rho\cos\phi\\ \text{Spherical to cylindrical:}\quad \amp r = \rho\sin\phi, \quad z = \rho\cos\phi, \quad \theta = \theta \end{align*}
    The corresponding formulas for \(dV\) in triple integrals (see (2.6.2) and (2.7.2)):
    \begin{equation*} dV = dx\,dy\,dz = dz\ r\,dr\,d\theta = \rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.2 Integration in Spherical Coordinates

    The volume element \(dV\) in spherical coordinates is illustrated in Figure 2.7.4: a spherical wedge with radial side \(d\rho\text{,}\) one arc side \(\rho\,d\phi\text{,}\) and the other arc side \(\rho\sin\phi\,d\theta\text{.}\) Say where each of the three edge lengths comes from, and multiply them to get \(dV\text{.}\)
    🔗

    The Spherical Volume Element.

    \begin{equation} dV = \rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.\tag{2.7.2} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    Then, the triple integral over a region \(D\) described in spherical coordinates by \(\alpha\le\theta\le\beta\text{,}\) \(\phi_{\min}\le\phi\le\phi_{\max}\text{,}\) and \(g_1(\phi,\theta)\le\rho\le g_2(\phi,\theta)\) can be converted into spherical coordinates as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \iiint_D f(x,y,z)\,dV = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\!\!\int_{\phi_{\min}}^{\phi_{\max}} \!\!\int_{g_1(\theta,\phi)}^{g_2(\theta,\phi)} f\!\left(\rho\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ \rho\sin\phi\sin\theta,\ \rho\cos\phi\right)\rho^2\sin\phi\ d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta.\tag{2.7.3} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.3 The Ice Cream Cone

    Example 2.7.5. Example III.

    Find the volume of the “ice cream cone” \(D\) cut from the solid sphere \(\rho \le 1\) by the cone \(\phi = \pi/3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Plot the ice cream cone, shown in Figure 2.7.6, and describe it by the ranges of \(\theta\text{,}\) \(\phi\) and \(\rho\text{.}\) Then use (2.5.2) and (2.7.3) with \(f = 1\text{,}\) and compare with Figure 2.7.7.)
    🔗
    🔗
    How the spherical description of the ice cream cone works—the angle \(\phi\) sweeping from the \(z\)-axis down to the cone while \(\rho\) reaches out to the sphere—is animated in Figure 2.7.7.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.4 Converting a Triple Integral to Spherical Coordinates

    Example 2.7.8. Example IV.

    Calculate the triple integral below by converting it to spherical coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-2}^{2}\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{4-x^2}}^{\sqrt{4-x^2}} \!\!\int_{2-\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}}^{2+\sqrt{4-x^2-y^2}} \left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2} dz\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution. (Read the solid off the limits: the \(z\)-limits are the two halves of a sphere; which one, and where is its center? It is shown in Figure 2.7.9. Convert its equation to spherical coordinates as in Example 2.7.2, and describe the solid by the ranges of \(\theta\text{,}\) \(\phi\) and \(\rho\text{;}\) the integrand is a power of \(\rho\text{.}\) Then apply (2.7.3); the \(\phi\)-integral is a substitution.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.7.5 Re-Writing an Integral in Spherical and Cylindrical Coordinates

    Example 2.7.10. Example V.

    Re-write (do not evaluate) the following integral in spherical and cylindrical coordinates:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-1}^{1}\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{1-x^2}}^{0} \!\!\int_{\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}^{1} \left(x^2+y^2\right) dz\,dy\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Solution. (Read the \(xy\)-projection off the outer two limits, shown in Figure 2.7.11, and describe it in polar coordinates. The \(z\)-limits run from a cone up to a plane; the solid is shown in Figure 2.7.12. For the cylindrical form, convert the cone and the integrand and use (2.6.3). For the spherical form, the cone is a fixed \(\phi\text{,}\) and the plane \(z = 1\) becomes an upper limit on \(\rho\) that depends on \(\phi\text{;}\) convert the integrand and use (2.7.3).)
    🔗
    🔗
