    Section 2.2 Double Integrals Over General Regions and Area by Double Integration

    Here we will generalize our last session’s discussion about double integrals and how they can be used to evaluate volumes. Last session we restricted ourselves to shapes with rectangular base. Today we will look at more general shapes for the base.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.1 Case I: Regions of the Form \(R = \{(x,y) : a \le x \le b,\ f_2(x) \le y \le f_1(x)\}\)

    Example 2.2.1. Example I.

    Calculate the following double integral,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R \left(2xy + 1\right)\,dx\,dy \end{equation*}
    where \(R = \{(x,y) : 0 \le x \le 1,\ (-2x^2+2x) \le y \le (x^3+1)\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (The region is shown in Figure 2.2.2. For each fixed \(x\text{,}\) \(y\) runs from the lower curve to the upper curve, so integrate along the vertical strips first: write the iterated integral with its limits, do the inner integral, and then the outer one.)

    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.2 Case II: Regions of the Form \(R = \{(x,y) : c \le y \le d,\ g_1(y) \le x \le g_2(y)\}\)

    Example 2.2.3. Example II.

    Calculate the following double integral,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R x^2 y\,dx\,dy \end{equation*}
    where \(R = \{(x,y) : 0.1 \le y \le 0.5,\ y^2 \le x \le y^{1/3}\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (The region is shown in Figure 2.2.4. This time, for each fixed \(y\text{,}\) \(x\) runs along a horizontal strip from the left curve to the right curve, so integrate with respect to \(x\) first.)
    🔗
    🔗
    The idea behind both cases—sweeping a strip across the region while the inner integral measures the length of the strip—is animated in Figure 2.2.5.

    Subsection 2.2.3 Volume of a Tetrahedron

    Example 2.2.6. Example III.

    Find the volume of the tetrahedron bounded by the planes \(x + 2y + z = 2\text{,}\) \(x = 2y\text{,}\) \(x = 0\) and \(z = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Figure 2.2.7 shows the tetrahedron. First find the region of integration, the base of the tetrahedron in the \(xy\)-plane: sketch it on Figure 2.2.8, with the lines that bound it and a typical vertical strip, and describe it in the form of Case I. Then the volume is the integral over that region of the height of the top plane, solved for \(z\text{.}\) Set up the iterated integral and evaluate.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 2.2.9. Why not integrate in the reverse order?

    We could also try to integrate with respect to \(x\) first, using horizontal strips. However, as the figure below shows, the right end of a horizontal strip depends on where the strip is: for \(y \le \tfrac12\) the strip ends on the line \(x = 2y\text{,}\) while for \(y \ge \tfrac12\) it ends on the line \(x + 2y = 2\) (that is, \(x = 2 - 2y\)). We would therefore have to split the region into two pieces and evaluate two double integrals, so integrating with respect to \(y\) first is the more efficient choice here.

    Subsection 2.2.4 Area by Double Integration

    In the previous session, we talked about finding the double integral (2.1.1) and how it corresponds to the volume of an object with base \(R\) and height \(z = f(x,y)\text{.}\) This session, we will look at a special case of this double integral, which corresponds to the area of region \(R\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \text{Area of region } R = \iint_R dx\,dy = \iint_R dA.\tag{2.2.1} \end{equation}

    Example 2.2.10. Example IV.

    Calculate the area of the region \(R\) shown in Figure 2.2.12, bounded on the right by \(x = y - y^3\text{,}\) on the left by \(x = -1\text{,}\) and by the parabola \(y = (x+1)^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Method 1. Split \(R\) at the \(x\)-axis into \(R_1\) below and \(R_2\) above, as in Figure 2.2.12, and describe each with horizontal strips; on \(R_2\) the left boundary is the parabola, solved for \(x\text{.}\) Then the area is (2.2.1) over each piece; the two outer integrals can be combined before evaluating.)
    🔗
    (Method 2, using symmetry. The curve \(x = y - y^3\) is odd, so rotating it by \(180^\circ\) about the origin maps it onto itself. In Figure 2.2.11, which piece does that rotation carry onto which? Swap them, so that the region becomes the unit square \([-1,0]\times[-1,0]\) together with the strip between the parabola and the \(y\)-axis, and add the two areas.)
    🔗

    Set up a double integral for computing the volume of the region bounded by the cylinder \(x^2+(y-1)^2=1\) and the sphere \(x^2+y^2+z^2=1\text{.}\) See Figure 2.2.14.
    🔗

The region of integration is the shadow of the solid, the set of points inside both circles \(x^2+y^2=1\) and \(x^2+(y-1)^2=1\text{;}\) find where the circles cross, and describe the region as in Subsection 2.2.1.)
