    Section 2.4 Double Integrals in Polar Form

    In the previous session, we talked about a new coordinate system known as polar coordinates. Today, we will use polar coordinates to calculate double integrals. As you will see, this set of coordinates can sometimes simplify our calculations. In Cartesian coordinates, double integrals are of the form (2.1.1),
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dx\,dy = \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 2.4.1 How Does a Double Integral Look in Polar Coordinates?

    To answer this question, assume that our region of integration is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R = \{(r,\theta) : a \le r \le b,\ \alpha \le \theta \le \beta\}, \end{equation*}
    which is shown on the left in Figure 2.4.1. We divide this region into polar rectangles as shown on the right. Then, we calculate the area \(\Delta A_i\) of a typical polar rectangle
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R_{ij} = \{(r,\theta) : r_{i-1} \le r \le r_i,\ \theta_{j-1} \le \theta \le \theta_j\}, \end{equation*}
    Compute this area. (The area of a sector of a circle with radius \(r\) and central angle \(\theta\) is \(\tfrac12 r^2\theta\text{;}\) the polar rectangle is the difference of two sectors. Factor the difference of squares and write the result in terms of \(\Delta r = r_i - r_{i-1}\text{,}\) \(\Delta\theta\text{,}\) and the average radius \(r_i^* = \tfrac12(r_i + r_{i-1})\text{.}\) Then write the Riemann sum for \(\iint_R f\,dA\) with these cells and pass to the limit.)
    🔗

    Double Integrals in Polar Coordinates.

    The area of a polar rectangle with average radius \(r_i^*\) is
    \begin{gather} \Delta A_i = r_i^*\,\Delta r\,\Delta\theta,\tag{2.4.1} \end{gather}
    so that \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\text{,}\) and
    \begin{gather} \iint_R f(x,y)\,dA = \iint_R f(r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta.\tag{2.4.2} \end{gather}
    🔗
    🔗
    The subdivision of \(R\) into polar rectangles, and how the area of a polar rectangle behaves as the grid is refined, is animated in Figure 2.4.2.

    Subsection 2.4.4 A Volume Computed in Polar Coordinates

    Example 2.4.7. Example III.

    Find the volume of the solid that lies under the paraboloid \(z = x^2 + y^2\text{,}\) above the \(xy\)-plane, and inside the cylinder \(x^2 + y^2 = 2x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Complete the square to see what the cylinder’s cross-section is; the base disk is shown in Figure 2.4.8 and the solid in Figure 2.4.9. Convert \(x^2+y^2 = 2x\) to polar form and solve for \(r\text{;}\) that gives the upper limit of \(r\text{,}\) and the disk is swept by \(-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2\text{.}\) Integrate the height of the paraboloid over the disk. The outer integral involves \(\cos^4\theta\text{:}\) use the half-angle identity twice, and use symmetry to halve the interval.)

    Subsection 2.4.6 Concept Check

    Each question below asks for a setup in polar coordinates. Recall from (2.3.1) that \(x = r\cos\theta\text{,}\) \(y = r\sin\theta\text{,}\) and from (2.4.1) that \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\text{,}\) so that the double integral takes the polar form (2.4.2).
    🔗
    The work is almost entirely in the limits: which values of \(\theta\) sweep the region, and for each such \(\theta\text{,}\) between which two values of \(r\) does the ray at that angle enter and leave the region. Two of the figures below carry a slider for \(\theta\) so you can watch the ray move.
