    Section 1.4 Vector Fields and Line Integrals

    In this section we meet two ideas. A vector field attaches a vector—a magnitude and a direction—to every point of the plane or of space; think of the velocity of the air at each point of a room, or the gravitational pull at each point around a planet. The line integral of a vector field then answers a natural question: as an object moves along a curve through the field, how much does the field push it along its way? The physical name for this accumulated push is work.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.1 Vector Fields

    What is a vector field? We will study the definition through an example. Assume that we have a function \(\mathbf{F}(x,y)\) on \(\R^2\text{:}\) the function \(\mathbf{F}\) takes two real numbers \((x,y)\) as its input and outputs a vector in \(\R^2\text{.}\) As an example, consider the following vector field:
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{F}(x,y)=\left\langle x,\ y\right\rangle.\tag{1.4.1} \end{gather}
    To visualize this vector field, imagine that at each point \((x,y)\) there is a vector \(\left\langle x,\ y\right\rangle\) attached. Physically, this vector could correspond to the velocity of an atom located at the point \((x,y)\text{.}\) See Figure 1.4.1.
    🔗
    What is the length of the vector in (1.4.1)? Compute it, and use it to explain why the blue arrows in Figure 1.4.1 grow in length as we move away from the origin along a straight line. Then modify the vector field so that the length of the vectors is one at every point. (Dividing a vector by its own length produces a unit vector.)
    🔗
    Visualized, the normalized field looks like Figure 1.4.2: the directions are unchanged, but now all arrows have the same length.
    🔗
    Our next example is a vector field which is a function on \(\R^3\text{.}\) Consider the following vector field:
    \begin{align} \mathbf{F}(x,y,z) \amp = \frac{\left\langle -x,\ -y,\ -z\right\rangle} {\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\notag\\ \amp = -\frac{\mathbf{r}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3}, \qquad \mathbf{r}=\left\langle x,\ y,\ z\right\rangle.\tag{1.4.2} \end{align}
    This field has the same dependence on \(x\text{,}\) \(y\) and \(z\) as the gravitational force exerted by a massive body located at the origin.
     1 
    The gravitational force exerted by a mass \(m_1\) at the origin on a mass \(m_2\) at the point \(\mathbf{r}\) is the gravitational force field \(\mathbf{F}_{\text{grav}} = -\dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3}\, \mathbf{r}\text{,}\) where \(G\) is the gravitational constant and the minus sign makes the force point back toward the origin. The field \(\mathbf{F}=-\mathbf{r}/\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert^3\) studied here is that force with the constant \(Gm_1m_2\) stripped off, so the two differ only by a positive factor and point the same way at every point.
    Every arrow points toward the origin. Compute \(\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\) in terms of \(\left\lVert\mathbf{r}\right\rVert\) to see how the pull weakens with distance. See Figure 1.4.3. We will meet this field again in Example 1.4.10.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.2 Work (Reminder)

    Our goal is to study the line integral of a vector field over a curve \(C\text{,}\) and we will do so using an example from physics: calculating the amount of work done by a force \(\mathbf{F}\text{,}\) which in general can be a function of \(x\text{,}\) \(y\text{,}\) and \(z\text{.}\) Before we present the example, let us remind ourselves of the definition of work. Consider a box being pulled by a constant force \(\mathbf{F}\text{,}\) as shown in Figure 1.4.4.
    🔗
    The amount of work done by the force is equal to the magnitude of the force in the direction of motion, \(\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\cos(\alpha)\text{,}\) multiplied by the amount of displacement \(\delta x\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather} \text{Work} =\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\cos(\alpha)\times\delta x =\mathbf{F}\cdot\left(\delta x\,\mathbf{i}\right).\tag{1.4.3} \end{gather}
    The last equality holds because the motion is in the \(+x\)-direction, which lets us rewrite \(\left\lVert\mathbf{F}\right\rVert\cos(\alpha)\times\delta x\) as the dot product \(\mathbf{F}\cdot(\delta x\,\mathbf{i})\text{.}\) This dot-product form is the one that generalizes: work is the dot product of the force with the displacement.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.3 Work of a Force Field

    Let us now consider the situation where a force \(\mathbf{F}(x,y)\) is acting on an object and causing it to move along a curved trajectory from an initial point to a final point. Neither the force nor the direction of motion is constant anymore, so we cannot apply (1.4.3) directly. Instead, we reuse the strategy of Section 1.3: calculate the work on small subarcs of the trajectory, where force and direction are nearly constant, and then sum up. Figure 1.4.5 shows one such subarc.
    🔗
    Build the work integral from the subarcs. (On the \(k\)th subarc the displacement has length \(\Delta s_k\) and direction \(\mathbf{T}_k\text{,}\) so the dot-product form of (1.4.3) gives the work done along it. Sum over the subarcs and let \(n \to \infty\text{.}\) Then use \(\mathbf{T} = \mathbf{v}/\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert = \frac{d\mathbf{r}/dt}{ds/dt}\) to rewrite \(\mathbf{T}\,ds\text{,}\) which gives the compact form of the integral, and finally write everything in terms of a parametrization \(\mathbf{r}(t)\text{,}\) \(a \le t \le b\text{.}\))
    🔗

    The Line Integral of a Vector Field.

    The work done by \(\mathbf{F}\) along \(C\) is approximated by
    \begin{gather} W\approx\sum_{k=1}^{n} \mathbf{F}(x_k,y_k)\cdot\mathbf{T}_k\,\Delta s_k,\tag{1.4.4} \end{gather}
    and exactly by
    \begin{gather} W = \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds,\tag{1.4.5} \end{gather}
    where \(\mathbf{T}\) is the unit tangent vector of the curve. Since
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{T}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} =\frac{\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}}{\frac{ds}{dt}} =\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{ds},\tag{1.4.6} \end{gather}
    the combination \(\mathbf{T}\,ds\) is simply \(d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) and
    \begin{gather} W=\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds =\int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}.\tag{1.4.7} \end{gather}
    In practice, once the curve is parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t)\) for \(a\le t\le b\text{,}\)
    \begin{gather} \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_a^b \mathbf{F}\bigl(\mathbf{r}(t)\bigr) \cdot\mathbf{v}(t)\,dt :\tag{1.4.8} \end{gather}
    evaluate the field along the curve, dot it with the velocity, and integrate over time.
    🔗
    🔗
    The applet below carries every ingredient of (1.4.8) at once: a curve \(C\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t)\text{,}\) the velocity \(\mathbf{v}(t)=d\mathbf{r}/dt\) tangent to it, and a force field \(\mathbf{F}\) evaluated at the moving point. Work out what the integrand \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}\) is doing before you compute anything.
    🔗
    Work was our motivation, but any vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) can be integrated over a curve \(C\) in this fashion; the number it produces is also called the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) along \(C\text{,}\) a name that becomes the usual one when \(C\) is a closed curve. Figure 1.4.7 shows the accumulation of \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds\) along a trajectory.
    🔗
    There is a third, frequently used way to write the same integral. Assume the vector field has components \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z)=\left\langle M(x,y,z),\ N(x,y,z),\ P(x,y,z)\right\rangle\text{.}\) Since \(d\mathbf{r}=\left\langle dx,\ dy,\ dz\right\rangle\text{,}\) expanding the dot product gives the differential form of the line integral:
    \begin{gather} \int_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r} =\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz.\tag{1.4.9} \end{gather}
    All three notations—(1.4.5), \(\int_C\mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) and \(\int_C M\,dx+N\,dy+P\,dz\)—mean exactly the same thing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.8. Work along a twisted cubic.

    Calculate the work done by the force
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y,z)=\left\langle y-x^2,\ z-y^2,\ x-z^2\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    over the curve \(C\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{r}(t)=\left\langle t,\ t^2,\ t^3\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    from \((0,0,0)\) to \((1,1,1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (Use (1.4.8). Read the parameter interval off the two endpoints, write the force along the curve by substituting \(x = t\text{,}\) \(y = t^2\text{,}\) \(z = t^3\) — one component vanishes identically — and differentiate for \(\mathbf{v}(t)\text{.}\) Dot, expand, and integrate term by term. The curve is shown in Figure 1.4.9.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.10. Work done by an inverse-square field along a helix.

    Calculate the line integral
    \begin{align*} \int_C \amp \frac{-x}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dx\\ \amp +\frac{-y}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dy\\ \amp +\frac{-z}{\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}}\,dz \end{align*}
    over the curve \(\mathbf{r}(t)=\langle\cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle\) from \((1,0,0)\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution. (This integral is in the differential form (1.4.9): identify \(\mathbf{F} = \langle M, N, P\rangle\) and recognize it as the inverse-square field (1.4.2) of Figure 1.4.3. Read off the parameter interval from the endpoints. Along the helix, simplify \(x^2+y^2+z^2\) first, then write \(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\) and \(\mathbf{v}(t)\text{;}\) the dot product collapses. Integrate with the substitution \(u = 1 + t^2\text{.}\) Say what the sign of the answer means physically, and check it against Figure 1.4.11 and Figure 1.4.12.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.13. The same endpoints, two paths, two answers.

    Calculate the work done by the force
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F}(x,y)=\left\langle y,\ 2x\right\rangle \end{equation*}
    in moving a particle from \(O(0,0)\) to \(P(1,1)\) along each of the following two paths:
    • \(C_1\text{,}\) the straight segment from \(O\) to \(P\text{,}\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}_1(t)=\left\langle t,\ t\right\rangle\text{,}\) \(0\le t\le 1\text{;}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(C_2\text{,}\) the arc of the parabola \(y=x^2\) from \(O\) to \(P\text{,}\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}_2(t)=\left\langle t,\ t^2\right\rangle\text{,}\) \(0\le t\le 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution. (Both curves start at \(O\) and end at \(P\text{,}\) as in Figure 1.4.14, so any difference between the two answers can come only from the paths themselves. Apply (1.4.8) to each: write \(\mathbf{F}\) along the path and the velocity, dot, and integrate. Compare \(W_1\) and \(W_2\text{,}\) and say what the comparison tells you about whether the endpoints alone determine the work. Which fields are the exception is the question taken up in Section 1.5.)

    Subsection 1.4.4 Concept Check

    Checkpoint 1.4.15. Positive, negative, or zero?

    In Figure 1.4.16, determine whether the work done by the force field along the two paths \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) is positive, negative, or zero.
    🔗
    (The field is the radial field (1.4.1), which aims straight away from the origin at every point. For the circle, ask what angle \(\mathbf{F}\) makes with the direction of travel \(\mathbf{T}\) at every point, and what that does to the integrand of (1.4.5). For the ellipse the integrand does not vanish: mark the stretches where the curve moves away from the origin and those where it moves back, decide the sign of \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\) on each, and think about what a full circuit adds up to. Does reversing the orientation change either answer?)
