    Section 3.3 The Divergence Theorem

    In Section 3.1 we saw that the divergence of a two-dimensional field measures its flux density, and Green’s theorem (in its normal form) converted a flux integral around a closed curve into a double integral of the divergence. In this section we extend both ideas to three dimensions: the result is Gauss’s divergence theorem, which converts a flux integral over a closed surface—often a lengthy parametrized computation, as in Section 2.10—into an ordinary triple integral.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.3.1 Divergence in 3D

    Recall from (3.1.2) that in 2D the divergence of \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) is \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \partial M/\partial x + \partial N/\partial y\text{,}\) the flux density of the field at a point: positive where the gas expands, negative where it is compressed (see Example 3.1.4). The three-dimensional definition adds one term.
    🔗

    Definition 3.3.1.

    Consider the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = M(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{j} + P(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Then the divergence of \(\mathbf{F}\) is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}.\tag{3.3.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    If the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) represents the velocity field of a flowing gas, then the divergence of \(\mathbf{F}\) corresponds to the amount of compression or expansion of the gas at the point \((x,y,z)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection 3.3.2 Gauss’s Divergence Theorem

    The divergence theorem relates the outward flux through the surface boundary of a 3D region to the triple integral over the volume of the enclosed region.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.3.5. Gauss’s Divergence Theorem.

    Let \(\mathbf{F}\) be a vector field whose components have continuous first partial derivatives, and let \(S\) be a piecewise smooth oriented closed surface. The flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(S\) in the direction of the surface’s outward unit normal field \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\) equals the integral of \(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\) over the region \(D\) enclosed by the surface:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \underbrace{\iint_S \mathbf{F} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma}_{\text{outward flux through the boundary } S} = \underbrace{\iiint_D \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F}\, dV}_{\text{triple integral of the divergence over } D}.\tag{3.3.2} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Proof.
    (Write \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j} + P\,\mathbf{k}\text{.}\) Both sides of (3.3.2) break into three pieces along the three components, so it is enough to prove
    \begin{align} \iint_S M\,\mathbf{i} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial M}{\partial x}\, dV ,\tag{3.3.3}\\ \iint_S N\,\mathbf{j} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}\, dV ,\notag\\ \iint_S P\,\mathbf{k} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma \amp = \iiint_D \frac{\partial P}{\partial z}\, dV ,\notag \end{align}
    and add them; prove the third, the other two being the same argument with the axes renamed. Step 1: suppose \(D\) is simple in the \(z\)-direction, as in Subsection 2.5.2, with floor \(z = u_1(x,y)\text{,}\) cap \(z = u_2(x,y)\) and shadow \(R\text{,}\) so that its boundary is the cap \(S_2\text{,}\) the floor \(S_1\) and the vertical wall \(S_3\) of Figure 3.3.6. Say why the wall contributes nothing. On the cap, use (2.10.6) for a graph to find \(\mathbf{k}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,d\sigma\text{;}\) on the floor the outward normal points the other way. Write the flux as two double integrals over \(R\text{.}\) Step 2: evaluate the triple integral of \(\partial P/\partial z\) by integrating in \(z\) first, as in (2.5.3), with the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus on the inner integral, and compare. Step 3: say what to do with a solid that is not simple in all three directions, and why the cut surfaces cancel; Figure 3.3.7 shows the idea.)
    🔗
    🔗
