    Section 1.3 Line Integrals

    In this section we study line integrals of scalar functions. We develop the idea through a concrete problem: finding the mass of a curved wire when we know its density—its mass per unit length—at every point. The strategy is the one behind every integral: chop the wire into tiny pieces, multiply the density of each piece by its length, and add up the results.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.1 Line Integrals of Scalar Functions

    Suppose we would like to compute the mass of a wire that does not have a trivial shape (such as a straight line). If the wire were straight and its density constant, the mass would simply be density times length. For a curved wire whose density \(\rho(x,y,z)\) varies from point to point, neither shortcut is available—but we can approximate mass of small portions of the wire by treating each as if it were straight and had constant density.
    🔗
    Assume the wire is represented by a curve \(C\) in space, parametrized by \(t\in[a,b]\text{,}\) as in Figure 1.3.1. Divide the wire into \(n\) small portions, and denote the length of the \(k\)th portion by \(\Delta s_k\text{.}\) Pick a sample point \((x_k,y_k,z_k)\) on this portion. If the portion is short, the density is nearly constant along it, so
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the } k\text{th portion} \approx \rule{6cm}{0.4pt}\tag{1.3.1} \end{gather}
    🔗
    and summing (1.3.1) over all portions,
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} \approx \rule{6cm}{0.4pt}\tag{1.3.2} \end{gather}
    🔗
    The estimate (1.3.2) is only an approximation, because the density is not actually constant over each portion. How can we make the calculation exact? We take the limit in which every portion becomes infinitely small, that is, \(\Delta s_k\to 0\text{,}\) or equivalently \(n\to\infty\text{:}\)
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} = \rule{6cm}{0.4pt}\tag{1.3.3} \end{gather}
    🔗
    The limit in (1.3.3) is denoted by the integral
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} = \rule{6cm}{0.4pt}\tag{1.3.4} \end{gather}
    🔗
    which is read as “the line integral of \(\rho\) over the curve \(C\text{.}\)” The process is animated in Figure 1.3.2: as the partition refines, the sums settle down to the value of the integral.
    🔗
    Our final task is to write the line element \(ds\) in terms of \(dt\text{,}\) so the integral becomes an ordinary integral we can evaluate.
    🔗

    Results.

    For a wire \(C\) parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle x(t), y(t), z(t)\right\rangle\text{,}\) \(a \le t \le b\text{,}\) with velocity \(\mathbf{v}(t) = d\mathbf{r}/dt\text{,}\) the line element is
    \begin{gather} ds = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt,\tag{1.3.5} \end{gather}
    🔗
    so the mass of the wire is
    \begin{gather} \text{mass of the wire} = \int_a^b \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt.\tag{1.3.6} \end{gather}
    🔗
    There is nothing special about density here. In general, a scalar line integral has the form
    \begin{gather} \int_C f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right)\,ds = \int_a^b f\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt,\tag{1.3.7} \end{gather}
    🔗
    🔗
    where \(f\) is a scalar-valued function and the curve \(C\) specifies the region of integration. Once the curve is parametrized in terms of \(t\text{,}\) the region of integration translates to \(a\le t\le b\text{.}\) In words: to compute a line integral, trade the geometric element \(ds\) for the parameter element \(\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\rVert\,dt\). Two sanity checks connect this to what we already know. Taking \(f=1\) in (1.3.7) recovers the arc length \(\int_C ds=\int_a^b \lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\rVert\,dt\) of (1.2.1)—total length is the “mass” of a wire of density one. And \(\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\rVert\) is exactly the speed from that section, so the factor converts time spent into distance covered.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.3. Mass and length of a helical wire.

    Consider a wire of length \(l\) and mass \(m\text{.}\) The wire is helix-shaped, with its bottom end located at \((1,0,0)\) and parametric equation
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{r}(t)=\langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle,\qquad t\ge 0.\tag{1.3.8} \end{gather}
    Its density (mass per unit length) is \(\rho(x,y,z)=4x^2y^2\) kg/m. Assuming that \(m=\dfrac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}\) kg, calculate the length \(l\) of the wire.
    🔗
    Solution. (The wire is the piece \(0 \le t \le b\) of the helix, where the upper limit \(b\) marks where the wire ends and is unknown. Plan: use the given mass in (1.3.6) to find \(b\text{,}\) then the length is the arc length up to \(b\text{.}\) Differentiate (1.3.8) for the speed; write the density along the curve in terms of \(t\) — the double-angle identity \(2\sin t\cos t = \sin(2t)\) and then the half-angle identity \(\sin^2 u = \tfrac12\left(1 - \cos(2u)\right)\) make the integral elementary. Solve the resulting equation for \(b\text{,}\) and say why the solution is the only one. Then compare with Figure 1.3.4 and Figure 1.3.5.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.6. A wire whose density is read off the picture.

    A wire of length \(40\) meters is parametrized as
    \begin{gather} \mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle t\cos(t),\ t\sin(t),\ \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}\,t^{3/2} \right\rangle ,\tag{1.3.9} \end{gather}
    🔗
    where \(x(t)\text{,}\) \(y(t)\) and \(z(t)\) are in meters and \(t\) is in seconds. Assume that the mass density at a point \(P\) of the wire is
    \begin{equation*} \rho = \frac{1}{d+1} \quad \text{kg/m} , \end{equation*}
    🔗
    where \(d\) is the distance from \(P\) to the \(z\)-axis.
    🔗
    The wire is graphed in Figure 1.3.7. Note that there is a piece of information that has to be extracted from the graph. Calculate the mass of the wire.
    🔗
    Solution. (Equation (1.3.9) is a formula for every \(t\text{,}\) but a wire is a finite piece of that curve: read off the graph where the wire starts, and say which \(t\) that is. The other end, \(t = b\text{,}\) is what the given length will pin down. Differentiate for the speed and simplify it all the way, as for the paraglider of Example 1.2.7. Set the arc length up to \(b\) equal to \(40\) and solve. Express \(d\text{,}\) and so \(\rho\text{,}\) in terms of \(t\text{.}\) Then compute the mass with (1.3.6); something cancels, and it is worth saying in words what that cancellation means for the wire.)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.3.2 Concept Check

    This last part of the section is not about computing anything new. It is about reviewing the concepts we have just built. Everything below refers to the two applications developed so far using line integrals, in particular the length \(L\) of a wire and its mass \(M\text{,}\)
    \begin{align} L \amp = \int_C ds = \int_{t=a}^{t=b}\left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt ,\tag{1.3.10}\\ M \amp = \int_C \rho\left(x,y,z\right) ds\notag\\ \amp = \int_{t=a}^{t=b} \rho\left(x(t),y(t),z(t)\right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}(t)\right\rVert\,dt ,\tag{1.3.11} \end{align}
    🔗
    where (1.3.11) is (1.3.6) again and (1.3.10) is the case \(\rho = 1\)—the arc length (1.2.1) once more. Assume throughout that the thickness of the wire can be ignored.
    🔗
