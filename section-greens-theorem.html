    Section 3.1 Green’s Theorem in the Plane

    Green’s theorem comes in two forms: one about how much of a vector field flows across a closed curve, and one about how much flows along it. Each needs an operation on vector fields of its own, divergence for the first and curl for the second. So the section has two halves. Each builds its operation, then the line integral it goes with, and then the form of Green’s theorem that connects the two when the curve is closed; the Concept Check at the end uses both.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.1 Flux and Divergence

    Subsubsection 3.1.1.1 Divergence

    Consider a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) to be the velocity field of a fluid. How can we find the net flow rate of the gas through the boundaries of a rectangle shown in Figure 3.1.1?
    🔗
    Find the net flow rate out of the rectangle, assuming the field is nearly constant along each side.
    🔗
    Note that this is an approximation, since we are assuming the velocity field has a constant value along each side of the rectangle. The flux density is
    🔗
    \begin{align} \amp \text{Flux density}\notag\\ \amp \quad = \frac{\text{net flux out of the rectangle}} {\text{area of the rectangle}}.\tag{3.1.1} \end{align}
    Note that, in the limit that \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) approach \(0\text{,}\) (3.1.1) represents to what extent a gas is expanding or compressing at each point. This quantity is known as the divergence, and it is denoted by \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}\text{.}\) Watch the construction in Figure 3.1.3.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.2.

    The divergence of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y},\tag{3.1.2} \end{equation}
    where \(\nabla = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\,\mathbf{i} + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.1.1.2 The Flux Across a Plane Curve

    The first form of Green’s theorem, in Subsubsection 3.1.1.3, measures how much of a field flows across a closed curve \(C\text{:}\) its outward flux \(\oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds\text{,}\) the line integral of the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) along the outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) of the curve. So it uses \(\mathbf{n}\) rather than the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\) of (1.4.6), introduced in Section 1.4. Suppose \(C\) is traversed counterclockwise and parametrized by \(\mathbf{r}(t) = x(t)\,\mathbf{i} + y(t)\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\) Its velocity, the tangent vector of (1.1.6), is
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt} = \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since \(\mathbf{n}\) is a unit normal to the curve, it is perpendicular to the tangent vector \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) so \(\mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The vector \(\left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle\) has this property, because
    \begin{align*} \amp \left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle \cdot \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle\\ \amp \quad = \frac{dy}{dt}\,\frac{dx}{dt} - \frac{dx}{dt}\,\frac{dy}{dt} = 0. \end{align*}
    It is \(\mathbf{v}\) turned a quarter turn clockwise. Of the two directions perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) this is the one that points out of the region: as \(C\) is traversed counterclockwise, the region \(R\) it encloses stays on the left, so turning \(\mathbf{v}\) clockwise makes it point to the right, out of \(R\text{.}\) (The opposite vector, \(\left\langle -\frac{dy}{dt},\ \frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle\text{,}\) points into \(R\text{.}\)) Therefore the outward normal is
    \begin{equation*} \left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    To find the unit normal we divide this vector by its length. Its length is \(\sqrt{\left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2} = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\text{,}\) the same as the length of \(\mathbf{v}\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation} \mathbf{n} = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left\langle \frac{dy}{dt},\ -\frac{dx}{dt} \right\rangle.\tag{3.1.3} \end{equation}
    Compare it with the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T} = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt} \right\rangle\) of (1.4.6): the two have the same components, swapped, with one sign changed.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.6. The Unit Normal to the Unit Circle.

    Find the outward unit normal \(\mathbf{n}\) and the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\) of the unit circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 1\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise.
    🔗
    Solution. (Compare with Figure 3.1.7.)
    🔗
    🔗
    This is where the line integral \(\oint_C M\,dy - N\,dx\) in the normal form of Green’s theorem, (3.1.4), comes from. For \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\text{,}\) the line element \(ds = \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\,dt\) of (1.2.2) cancels the length in (3.1.3):
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{n}\,ds\\ \amp \quad = \frac{1}{\left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert} \left( M\,\frac{dy}{dt} - N\,\frac{dx}{dt} \right) \left\lVert\mathbf{v}\right\rVert\,dt\\ \amp \quad = M\,dy - N\,dx. \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.1.1.3 Green’s Theorem: The Flux-Divergence (Normal) Form

    We can now state Green’s theorem in 2D
     1 
    Note that we only cover Green’s theorem in 2D; however, you should know that this theorem can be generalized to 3D, and in that case it is known as Stokes’ theorem, which is Section 3.2.
    . It comes in two forms, and the first, the flux form, relates the outward flux across a simple
     2 
    A curve in the \(xy\)-plane is simple if it does not cross itself.
    closed curve \(C\) and the double integral of the divergence over the region \(R\) bounded by the curve \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.8. Green’s Theorem (Flux-Divergence or Normal Form).

    Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve enclosing a region \(R\) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) be a vector field with \(M\) and \(N\) having continuous first partial derivatives in an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(C\) equals the double integral of \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}\) over the region \(R\) enclosed by \(C\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align} \amp \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}\, ds}_{\text{outward flux}} = \oint_C M\, dy - N\, dx\notag\\ \amp \quad = \underbrace{\iint_R \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy}_{\substack{\text{integral of} \\ \operatorname{div}\mathbf{F} \text{ over } R}}.\tag{3.1.4} \end{align}
    🔗

    Example 3.1.9. Verifying the Normal Form on the Unit Circle.

    Subsection 3.1.2 Circulation and Curl

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.1 Curl

    Again, let us consider a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) to be the velocity field of a fluid. This time we are interested in answering this question: how can we figure out the circulation density of the fluid at any point on the \(xy\)-plane?
    🔗
    To answer this question, we compute the flow rates of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) along the sides of the rectangle (counterclockwise), as in Figure 3.1.11, and then we take the limit as \(\Delta x, \Delta y \to 0\) to find the circulation density at a point in the \(xy\)-plane.
    🔗
    Find the circulation density for the rectangle of Figure 3.1.11.
    🔗
    This quantity is denoted by \(\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{k}\) and is known as the \(k\)-component of curl.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.12.

    The \(k\)-component of the curl of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{k} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}.\tag{3.1.5} \end{equation}
    🔗
    To find out the circulation density at a point in the \(xy\)-plane, you can imagine a paddle wheel whose axis goes through the point of interest. Then the \(k\)-component of curl is positive if the fluid flow causes the paddle wheel to rotate counterclockwise, and it is negative if it turns clockwise. The animation in Figure 3.1.13 shows both behaviors.

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.2 The Circulation Around a Plane Curve

    The second form of Green’s theorem, in Subsubsection 3.1.2.3, measures how much of a field flows along a closed curve \(C\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise. That is the line integral of a vector field from Subsection 1.4.3: the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) along the unit tangent \(\mathbf{T}\) of (1.4.6), integrated with respect to arc length. Around a closed curve it is called the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\text{.}\) Since \(\mathbf{T}\,ds = d\mathbf{r}\text{,}\) for \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) it is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{T}\,ds \amp = \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp = \oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy, \end{align*}
    the differential form of (1.4.9).
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.3 Green’s Theorem: The Circulation-Curl (Tangential) Form

    The second form of Green’s theorem connects the counterclockwise circulation of a vector field around a simple closed curve \(C\) and the double integral of the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl of the field over the region \(R\) enclosed by the curve.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.16. Green’s Theorem (Circulation-Curl or Tangential Form).

    Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve enclosing a region \(R\) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) be a vector field with \(M\) and \(N\) having continuous first partial derivatives in an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then the counterclockwise circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\) equals the double integral of \((\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k}\) over \(R\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align} \amp \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds}_{\text{circulation}} = \oint_C M\, dx + N\, dy\notag\\ \amp \quad = \underbrace{\iint_R \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy}_{\substack{\text{integral of} \\ \operatorname{curl}\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{k} \text{ over } R}}.\tag{3.1.6} \end{align}
    🔗

    Subsubsection 3.1.2.4 An Alternative Form of the Component Test for Conservative Fields

    Recall the component test for conservative fields:
    🔗

    Reminder: Component Test for Conservative Fields.

    Let \(\mathbf{F} = M(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{j} + P(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{k}\) be a field on a connected and simply connected domain whose component functions have continuous first partial derivatives. Then \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative if and only if
    🔗
    🔗
    The three equalities of (3.1.7) are equivalent to \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) since
    🔗
    \begin{align} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ M \amp N \amp P \end{vmatrix}\notag\\ \amp = \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial N}{\partial z} \right) \mathbf{i}\notag\\ \amp \quad {} + \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} \right) \mathbf{j}\notag\\ \amp \quad {} + \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) \mathbf{k}.\tag{3.1.8} \end{align}
    So to check whether a vector field is conservative or not, we can check whether its curl is zero or not.
    🔗
