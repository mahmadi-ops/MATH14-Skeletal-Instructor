    Section 3.1 Green’s Theorem in the Plane

    Before we learn about Green’s theorem, we will need to learn about two operations on vector fields: curl and divergence. We begin by studying divergence.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.1 Divergence

    Consider a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) to be the velocity field of a fluid. How can we find the net flow rate of the gas through the boundaries of a rectangle shown in Figure 3.1.1?
    🔗
    Evaluate the flux through each side, assuming the field is nearly constant along each side: on each side take the outward component of \(\mathbf{F}\) times the length of the side, with \(\mathbf{F}\) evaluated at the side’s corner shown in the figure. Then add the four contributions, pair the parallel sides, and use the linear approximations \(M(x+\Delta x, y) - M(x,y) \approx \frac{\partial M}{\partial x}\,\Delta x\) and \(N(x, y+\Delta y) - N(x,y) \approx \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}\,\Delta y\) to write the net flow rate.
    🔗
    Note that this is an approximation, since we are assuming the velocity field has a constant value along each side of the rectangle. The flux density can be calculated as follows:
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \text{Flux density} = \frac{\text{net flux through the rectangle}} {\text{area of the rectangle}} = \frac{\left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) \Delta x \Delta y} {\Delta x \Delta y} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y}.\tag{3.1.1} \end{equation}
    Note that, in the limit that \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\) approach \(0\text{,}\) (3.1.1) represents to what extent a gas is expanding or compressing at each point. This quantity is known as the divergence, and it is denoted by \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}\text{.}\) Watch the construction in Figure 3.1.3.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.2.

    The divergence of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) is defined as
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y},\tag{3.1.2} \end{equation}
    where \(\nabla = \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\,\mathbf{i} + \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\,\mathbf{j}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.2 Curl

    Again, let us consider a vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) to be the velocity field of a fluid. This time we are interested in answering this question: how can we figure out the circulation density of the fluid at any point on the \(xy\)-plane?
    🔗
    To answer this question, we compute the flow rates of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}\) along the sides of the rectangle (counterclockwise), as in Figure 3.1.6, and then we take the limit as \(\Delta x, \Delta y \to 0\) to find the circulation density at a point in the \(xy\)-plane.
    🔗
    Compute the flow rate along each edge, taking the component of \(\mathbf{F}\) in the direction of travel (counterclockwise) times the length of the edge, with \(\mathbf{F}\) evaluated at the corner shown in the figure. Sum the flows along the two pairs of parallel edges, using the same linear approximations as for the divergence, and divide by the area of the rectangle to get the circulation density.
    🔗
    This quantity is denoted by \(\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{k}\) and is known as the \(k\)-component of curl.
    🔗

    Definition 3.1.7.

    The \(k\)-component of the curl of the vector field \(\mathbf{F}(x,y) = M(x,y)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y)\,\mathbf{j}\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{k} = \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}.\tag{3.1.3} \end{equation}
    🔗
    To find out the circulation density at a point in the \(xy\)-plane, you can imagine a paddle wheel whose axis goes through the point of interest. Then the \(k\)-component of curl is positive if the fluid flow causes the paddle wheel to rotate counterclockwise, and it is negative if it turns clockwise. The animation in Figure 3.1.8 shows both behaviors.
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.3 The Two Forms of Green’s Theorem

    Next we state Green’s theorem in 2D
     1 
    Note that we only cover Green’s theorem in 2D; however, you should know that this theorem can be generalized to 3D, and in that case it is known as Stokes’ theorem, which is Section 3.2.
    , in two forms. The first form relates the outward flux across a simple
     2 
    A curve in the \(xy\)-plane is simple if it does not cross itself.
    closed curve \(C\) and the double integral of the divergence over the region \(R\) bounded by the curve \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.11. Green’s Theorem (Flux-Divergence or Normal Form).

    Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve enclosing a region \(R\) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) be a vector field with \(M\) and \(N\) having continuous first partial derivatives in an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then the outward flux of \(\mathbf{F}\) across \(C\) equals the double integral of \(\operatorname{div} \mathbf{F}\) over the region \(R\) enclosed by \(C\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{n}\, ds = \oint_C M\, dy - N\, dx}_{\text{outward flux through the boundary}} = \underbrace{\iint_R \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial N}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy}_{\text{double integral of divergence over } R}.\tag{3.1.4} \end{equation}
    🔗
    The second form of Green’s theorem connects the counterclockwise circulation of a vector field around a simple closed curve \(C\) and the double integral of the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of the curl of the field over the region \(R\) enclosed by the curve.
    🔗

    Theorem 3.1.12. Green’s Theorem (Circulation-Curl or Tangential Form).

    Let \(C\) be a piecewise smooth, simple closed curve enclosing a region \(R\) in the plane. Let \(\mathbf{F} = M\,\mathbf{i} + N\,\mathbf{j}\) be a vector field with \(M\) and \(N\) having continuous first partial derivatives in an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then the counterclockwise circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around \(C\) equals the double integral of \((\operatorname{curl} \mathbf{F}) \cdot \mathbf{k}\) over \(R\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \underbrace{\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{T}\, ds = \oint_C M\, dx + N\, dy}_{\text{counterclockwise circulation}} = \underbrace{\iint_R \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) dx\, dy}_{\text{double integral of curl} \,\cdot\, \mathbf{k} \text{ over } R}.\tag{3.1.5} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Proof.

    (Both sides of (3.1.5) split into a piece carrying \(M\) and a piece carrying \(N\text{,}\) so it is enough to prove the two statements
    \begin{align} \oint_C M\,dx \amp = -\iint_R \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\,dA ,\tag{3.1.6}\\ \oint_C N\,dy \amp = \iint_R \frac{\partial N}{\partial x}\,dA ,\notag \end{align}
    and add them. Step 1: for a region of the kind in Subsection 2.2.1, shown in Figure 3.1.13, say why the vertical sides contribute nothing to \(\oint_C M\,dx\text{,}\) write the bottom and top contributions as integrals in \(x\) (mind the direction of travel along the top), and compare with the double integral done in the \(y\)-direction first, where the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus evaluates the inner integral. Step 2: read \(R\) the other way, as in Subsection 2.2.2, and repeat for \(N\text{;}\) say why no minus sign appears this time. Step 3: say what to do with a region that cannot be described both ways, and why the cuts cancel; Figure 3.1.14 shows the idea. Finally, explain how Theorem 3.1.11 follows by applying the tangential form to the field \(\langle -N, M\rangle\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.1.4 An Alternative Form of the Component Test for Conservative Fields

    Recall the component test for conservative fields:
    🔗

    Reminder: Component Test for Conservative Fields.

    Let \(\mathbf{F} = M(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{i} + N(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{j} + P(x,y,z)\,\mathbf{k}\) be a field on a connected and simply connected domain whose component functions have continuous first partial derivatives. Then \(\mathbf{F}\) is conservative if and only if
    🔗
    🔗
    The three equalities of (3.1.7) are equivalent to \(\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \mathbf{0}\text{,}\) since
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \nabla \times \mathbf{F} = \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf{i} \amp \mathbf{j} \amp \mathbf{k} \\ \dfrac{\partial}{\partial x} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial y} \amp \dfrac{\partial}{\partial z} \\ M \amp N \amp P \end{vmatrix} = \left( \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial N}{\partial z} \right) \mathbf{i} + \left( \frac{\partial M}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} \right) \mathbf{j} + \left( \frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y} \right) \mathbf{k}.\tag{3.1.8} \end{equation}
    So to check whether a vector field is conservative or not, we can check whether its curl is zero or not.
    🔗
