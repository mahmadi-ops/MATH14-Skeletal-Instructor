Example 3.2.12. A Term That Cannot Be Integrated.

Evaluate \(\oint_C \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}\) for
\begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = \left[\cos\left(x^{100}\right) + 3y\right]\mathbf{i} + 4z\,\mathbf{j} + 2y\,\mathbf{k}, \end{equation*}
where \(C\) is the circle \(x^2 + y^2 = 9\text{,}\) \(z = 2\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise when seen from above.
🔗
Solution. (The term \(\cos(x^{100})\) makes a direct evaluation hopeless, but Stokes’ theorem only ever sees the field through its curl: compute it by (3.2.1) and watch the monster disappear. Take the cheapest surface with boundary \(C\text{,}\) the flat disk of Figure 3.2.13; which unit normal does the right-hand rule pair with the counterclockwise orientation? The integrand is then a constant.)
