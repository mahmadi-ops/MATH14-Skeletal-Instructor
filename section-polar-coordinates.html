    Section 2.3 Polar Coordinates and Graphing Polar Equations

    Usually we use Cartesian coordinates, which are directed distances from two perpendicular axes. Here we will study a new coordinate system, called the polar coordinate system, which is more convenient for many purposes such as finding the volume of some objects.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.1 The Polar Coordinate System

    Similar to the Cartesian coordinate system, we need two real numbers to specify the location of a point \(P\) in the \(xy\)-plane. The first one is the directed distance from the origin and it is shown by \(r\) in Figure 2.3.1. The second one is the directed angle \(\theta\text{,}\) and an angle is positive if measured in the counterclockwise direction from the positive \(x\)-axis and negative in the clockwise direction. The ordered pair \((r,\theta)\) represents the location of a point \(P\) in the polar coordinate system.
    🔗
    Figure 2.3.2 demonstrates the meaning of negative \(r\text{.}\) Note that the polar pair \((-r,\theta)\) is the same as \((r,\theta+\pi)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.2 Relation Between Polar and Cartesian Coordinates

    From the right triangle in Figure 2.3.3, write \(x\) and \(y\) in terms of \((r,\theta)\text{,}\) and then \(r\) and \(\theta\) in terms of \((x,y)\text{.}\) (Given \((r,\theta)\) the point \((x,y)\) is unique; given \((x,y) \ne (0,0)\text{,}\) how many choices are there for \(r\text{,}\) and how many for \(\theta \in [0,2\pi)\) once \(r\) is chosen?)
    🔗

    Equations Relating Polar and Cartesian Coordinates.

    \begin{equation} x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{y}{x}\tag{2.3.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.3 Non-Uniqueness of Polar Coordinates

    Unlike Cartesian coordinates, polar coordinates are not unique: a point has many representations. For example, in Figure 2.3.4 you can see two different representations, \(\left(1,\tfrac{13\pi}{4}\right)\) and \(\left(-1,\tfrac{\pi}{4}\right)\text{,}\) of the same point; the pairs \(\left(1,\tfrac{5\pi}{4}\right)\) and \(\left(1,-\tfrac{3\pi}{4}\right)\) locate it as well. In general, a point denoted by \((r,\theta)\) can also be represented by \((r,\theta + 2n\pi)\) or \((-r,\theta + (2n+1)\pi)\text{,}\) where \(n\) is any integer, and all the representations of a point can be generated this way from any one of them.
    🔗
    🔗

    Next, we would like to learn about graphing polar equations. We will begin by analyzing symmetries of polar equations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.5 Symmetries

    Symmetry about the \(x\)-axis.

    Note that a graph is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis if for every point \((r,\theta)\) on the graph, there exists a point with the coordinates \((r,-\theta)\) on the graph as well. As an example, consider the equation \(r = 1 + \cos\theta\text{.}\) Explain why, if \((r,\theta)\) is a point on the graph of this equation, so is \((r,-\theta)\text{.}\) Then plot its graph: fill in Table 2.3.7, mark the points on the polar grid below, use the symmetry to complete the curve, and name it. The process of sweeping \(\theta\) to trace the graph is animated in Figure 2.3.8; use it to check your sketch.
    🔗
    🔗

    Symmetry about the \(y\)-axis.

    For every point \((r,\theta)\) on the graph, there exists a point with the coordinates \((-r,-\theta)\) or \((r,\pi-\theta)\) on the graph. As an example, consider \(r = 1 - \sin(\theta)\text{,}\) which is shown in Figure 2.3.9: since \(\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta\text{,}\) the graph is symmetric about the \(y\)-axis. The symmetry is animated in Figure 2.3.10.
    🔗
    🔗

    Symmetry about the origin.

    If the point \((r,\theta)\) is on the graph, then the point \((-r,\theta)\) or \((r,\theta+\pi)\) lies on the graph as well. As an example, \(r^2 = \sin\theta\text{,}\) shown in Figure 2.3.11, is symmetric about the origin: its graph consists of the two branches \(r = \sqrt{\sin\theta}\) and \(r = -\sqrt{\sin\theta}\text{,}\) and replacing \(r\) by \(-r\) leaves the equation unchanged. The two branches are traced simultaneously in Figure 2.3.12.
