    Section 1.5 Path Independence, Conservative Fields, and Potential Functions

    Before we begin discussing the actual topic of today’s lecture, we will need to study a particular type of a vector field known as a gradient field.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.5.1 A Brief Reminder (Gradient Fields)

    Definition 1.5.1. Gradient Field.

    The gradient field of a differentiable function \(f(x,y,z)\) is the field of gradient vectors
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\mathbf i + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\mathbf j + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\mathbf k.\tag{1.5.1} \end{equation}
    🔗
    At each point \((x,y,z)\text{,}\) the gradient field gives a vector pointing in the direction of greatest increase of \(f\text{.}\) Let us emphasize that even though the function \(f\) is a scalar-valued function, its gradient is a vector-valued one by definition. As an example, see Figure 1.5.2.
    🔗
    In Figure 1.5.2, you see the scalar-valued function \(z = f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\text{,}\) which is a function on \(\R^2\text{.}\) Compute its gradient according to (1.5.1), and compare with the arrows in the figure.
    🔗
    The gradient field is visualized in the \(xy\)-plane and the value of the function \(f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\) at each point \((x,y)\) is plotted on the \(z\)-axis. Intuitively, the gradient of a function \(f(x,y)\) at a point \((x_0,y_0)\) is a vector pointing in the direction of the steepest slope.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.5.2 Path Independence and Some Basic Definitions

    Previously, in Example 1.4.13, we saw that the line integral of a vector field depends on the path in general. Then, the natural question to ask is:
    🔗
    What are the conditions under which the line integral of a vector field \(\mathbf F\) from point \(A\) to point \(B\) is independent of the curve \(C\) taken to go from \(A\) to \(B\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Answering this question requires a few new ideas, so we introduce them first.
    🔗

    Remark 1.5.3. Reminder.

    A smooth curve has no sharp corners or cusps; when the tangent vector turns, it does so continuously.
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 1.5.4. Open, Connected, and Simply Connected Regions; Conservative Fields.

    • A region \(D\) is open if for every point \(P\) in \(D\) there is a disk with center \(P\) that lies entirely in \(D\text{,}\) which means that \(D\) doesn’t contain any of its boundary points.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Consider an open region \(D\) in space and a vector field \(\mathbf F\text{.}\) If the result of the line integral \(\int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) from \(A\) to \(B\) is the same for all possible paths inside \(D\text{,}\) then the line integral \(\int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) is said to be path-independent inside \(D\) and the field \(\mathbf F\) is said to be conservative on \(D\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • If a vector field \(\mathbf F\) can be written as \(\mathbf F = \nabla f\text{,}\) where \(f\) is a scalar-valued function, then \(f\) is a potential function.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Piecewise smooth curves are curves that are made up of finitely many smooth pieces.
      🔗
      🔗
    • An open region \(D\) is said to be connected if any two points in \(D\) can be joined by a smooth curve that lies in the region.
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(D\) is simply connected, which means that every loop in \(D\) can be contracted to a point in \(D\) without ever leaving \(D\text{.}\) (See Figure 1.5.5.)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.5.3 Line Integrals in Conservative Fields

    Next, we will study the line integrals of conservative fields, in the sense of Definition 1.5.4. As you will recognize, if you think of the gradient of a vector field as its derivative, then the fundamental theorem of line integrals makes a similar statement for vector-valued functions as the one made for definite integrals of scalar-valued functions, i.e., the one that you know as FTC II.
     1 
    The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part II: if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\) on that interval, then \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)\text{.}\) It evaluates a definite integral from the values of an antiderivative at the two endpoints, and Theorem 1.5.6 does the same for a line integral, using the values of a potential function at the two endpoints of the curve.
    🔗

    Theorem 1.5.6. Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals.

    Let \(C\) be a smooth curve joining the point \(A\) to the point \(B\) in the plane or in space and parametrized by \(\mathbf r(t)\text{.}\) Let \(f\) be a differentiable function with a continuous gradient vector \(\mathbf F = \nabla f\) on a domain \(D\) containing \(C\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \int_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = f(B) - f(A).\tag{1.5.2} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Proof.

    (Parametrize \(C\) by \(\mathbf r(t) = x(t)\mathbf i + y(t)\mathbf j + z(t)\mathbf k\text{,}\) \(a \le t \le b\text{,}\) and write the line integral in the parametrized form (1.4.8) with \(\mathbf F = \nabla f\text{.}\) Expand the dot product of the gradient with \(d\mathbf r/dt\text{;}\) the chain rule turns the integrand into a single derivative with respect to \(t\text{,}\) and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus finishes the job.)
    🔗
    🔗
    In Figure 1.5.7, the region \(D\) is drawn as a translucent solid and the curve \(C\) is in red. According to the fundamental theorem, the line integral of a conservative vector field \(\mathbf F\) can be easily calculated by calculating the values of its corresponding potential function \(f\) (Definition 1.5.4) at the beginning (point \(A\)) and end (point \(B\)) of the curve \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The central idea of the theorem—that only the endpoints matter—is animated in Figure 1.5.8.
    🔗
    In the next example, we will see how we can use Theorem 1.5.6.
    🔗

    Example 1.5.9. Example I.

    Consider the scalar-valued function \(f(x,y,z) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Calculate the gradient vector field \(\mathbf F(x,y,z) = \nabla f\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Evaluate the line integral of the vector field \(\mathbf F(x,y,z) = \nabla f\) over the curve \(C_1\) parametrized as \(\mathbf r(t) = \cos(t)\mathbf i + \sin(t)\mathbf j + t\mathbf k\) from \((1,0,0)\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    3. What is the result of \(\int_{C_2} \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r\) from \((1,0,0)\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\) where \(C_2\) is the line connecting the two points?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution. (In A, write \(f = \left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{-1/2}\) and take the three partial derivatives; you should recognize the field. In B, one way would be to parametrize, exactly as in Example 1.4.10; instead use Theorem 1.5.6, which needs only the values of \(f\) at the two endpoints. In C, say what Theorem 1.5.6 tells you about \(C_2\) without computing anything, and why. The two curves are shown in Figure 1.5.10.)

    Subsection 1.5.4 Finding a Potential Function

    Example 1.5.11. Example II.

    Consider the vector field \(\mathbf F = (2xy + 3)\mathbf i + (x^2 - 4y)\mathbf j\) in the plane.
    🔗
    1. Find a function \(f\) such that \(\mathbf F = \nabla f\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Use the solution of part (A) to calculate the line integral of \(\mathbf F\) along any curve from \((0,0)\) to \((1,2)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution. (In A, match the two components of \(\mathbf F\) with \(\partial f/\partial x\) and \(\partial f/\partial y\text{,}\) one at a time. Step I: integrate the first with respect to \(x\text{,}\) remembering that the “constant” of integration may depend on \(y\text{.}\) Step II: differentiate what you have with respect to \(y\text{,}\) compare with the second component, and solve for that function of \(y\text{.}\) In B, say why Theorem 1.5.6 applies to any curve from \((0,0)\) to \((1,2)\text{,}\) then evaluate. What happens to the arbitrary constant?)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.12. Example III.

    Consider the vector field \(\mathbf F = yz\,\mathbf i + xz\,\mathbf j + (xy + 2z)\mathbf k\text{.}\)
    🔗
    1. Find a function \(f\) such that \(\mathbf F = \nabla f\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Use the solution of part (A) to calculate the line integral from \((1,0,-2)\) to \((4,6,3)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution. (In A, match the three components one at a time. Step I: integrate the first with respect to \(x\text{;}\) the constant of integration may now depend on \(y\) and \(z\text{.}\) Step II: differentiate with respect to \(y\) and compare with the second component; what does that say about the dependence on \(y\text{?}\) Step III: differentiate with respect to \(z\text{,}\) compare with the third component, and solve. In B, apply Theorem 1.5.6 with \(A(1,0,-2)\) and \(B(4,6,3)\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.5.13. Example IV.

    In Example 1.4.10 we computed the work done by the inverse-square field
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf F = \frac{\langle -x,\,-y,\,-z\rangle} {\left(x^2+y^2+z^2\right)^{3/2}} = -\frac{\mathbf r}{\lVert\mathbf r\rVert^{3}} \end{equation*}
    along the helix \(\mathbf r(t) = \langle \cos t,\ \sin t,\ t\rangle\) from \((1,0,0)\) to \((1,0,2\pi)\text{,}\) by parametrizing the curve. Do it again with Theorem 1.5.6.
    🔗
    Solution. (First say why looking for a potential function is legitimate here: where is the field defined, and does the helix avoid that point? Rather than run the three steps of Example 1.5.12, notice that \(\mathbf F\) depends only on the distance \(\rho = \lVert\mathbf r\rVert = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}\text{,}\) and that \(\partial\rho/\partial x = x/\rho\text{,}\) and likewise for \(y\) and \(z\text{.}\) Guess \(f = 1/\rho\) and check \(\nabla f\) with the chain rule. Then the work needs nothing but \(\rho\) at the two endpoints. Compare with Example 1.4.10, and say what this calculation never used.)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.5.5 Conservative Fields are Gradient Fields

    Theorem 1.5.14. Conservative Fields are Gradient Fields.

    Let \(\mathbf F = M\mathbf i + N\mathbf j + P\mathbf k\) be a vector field whose components are continuous throughout an open connected region \(D\) in space (Definition 1.5.4). Then \(\mathbf F\) is conservative if and only if \(\mathbf F\) is a gradient field \(\nabla f\) for a differentiable function \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    (Gradient implies conservative: assume \(\mathbf F = \nabla f\) and apply Theorem 1.5.6 to any piecewise smooth curve from \(A\) to \(B\text{;}\) what does the answer depend on? Conservative implies gradient: fix a point \(A_0\) of \(D\) and define \(f(x,y,z)\) as the line integral of \(\mathbf F\) from \(A_0\) to \((x,y,z)\) along any curve in \(D\text{;}\) say why connectedness and path independence make this definition meaningful. To show \(\partial f/\partial x = M\text{,}\) compare \(f(x+h,y,z)\) with \(f(x,y,z)\) by travelling along the straight segment \(L_h\) from \((x,y,z)\) to \((x+h,y,z)\text{,}\) which lies in \(D\) because \(D\) is open; parametrize \(L_h\text{,}\) so that the difference quotient becomes an average of \(M\) over the segment, and let \(h \to 0\) using the continuity of \(M\text{.}\) The other two partials are the same argument.)
    🔗
    🔗
    Path independence means that all of the paths in Figure 1.5.15 give the same value of the line integral: if \(\mathbf F\) is conservative, then
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \int_{C_1} \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_{C_2} \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \int_{C_3} \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = f(B) - f(A).\tag{1.5.3} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Subsection 1.5.6 The Loop Property of Conservative Fields

    Theorem 1.5.16. Loop Property of Conservative Fields.

    The following statements are equivalent.
    🔗
    1. \(\oint_C \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = 0\) around every loop (that is, closed curve \(C\)) in \(D\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    2. The field \(\mathbf F\) is conservative on \(D\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    ((2) implies (1): split any loop as \(C = C_1 \cup C_2\text{,}\) with \(C_1\) from \(A\) to \(B\) and \(C_2\) from \(B\) back to \(A\text{,}\) as in Figure 1.5.17. Reversing \(C_2\) gives a second path \(-C_2\) from \(A\) to \(B\text{;}\) what does path independence say about \(\int_{C_1}\) and \(\int_{-C_2}\text{,}\) and how is \(\int_{-C_2}\) related to \(\int_{C_2}\text{?}\) Add up. (1) implies (2): take any two paths \(C_1\text{,}\) \(C_2\) from \(A\) to \(B\text{,}\) build a loop out of them, and apply the assumption to it.)
    🔗
    🔗
    We can now collect everything this section has proved. The content of Theorem 1.5.14, combined with Theorem 1.5.6, the loop property in Theorem 1.5.16, and the definition of path independence in Definition 1.5.4, can be organized into the following square of equivalent statements. Throughout, \(\mathbf F\) is a continuous field with continuous partial derivatives on an open, connected, simply connected region \(D\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Read across the top: a conservative field is exactly a gradient field, and the potential function \(f\) is what makes that concrete. Read across the bottom: path independence and the loop property are two ways of saying the same thing, and the Fundamental Theorem turns either one into a number, \(f(B) - f(A)\text{.}\) Read down either side: each statement on the top implies, and is implied by, the one beneath it. In practice the four are used in whichever direction is convenient—find a potential function to evaluate a line integral, or observe that a field is a gradient field to conclude at once that its integral around every loop vanishes.
    🔗
