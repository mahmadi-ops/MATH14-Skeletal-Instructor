    1.

    A two-dimensional object has the shape given by \(r = \cos\theta\) and density \(\delta(r,\theta) = r + 1\text{.}\) Set up (do not evaluate) all the integrals required to compute the center of mass of this object.
    🔗

    Solution.

    The curve \(r = \cos\theta\) is a circle: multiplying by \(r\) gives \(r^2 = r\cos\theta\text{,}\) that is,
    \begin{align*} \amp x^2 + y^2 = x\\ \amp \Longleftrightarrow\ \left(x - \tfrac12\right)^2 + y^2 = \tfrac14 , \end{align*}
    the circle of radius \(\frac12\) centered at \(\left(\frac12, 0\right)\) (Figure 4.13.1). The object is the disk it bounds, swept out by
    \begin{gather*} 0 \le r \le \cos\theta,\\ -\frac{\pi}{2} \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2} . \end{gather*}
    🔗
    In polar coordinates \(dA = r\,dr\,d\theta\text{,}\) \(x = r\cos\theta\) and \(y = r\sin\theta\text{.}\) The mass and the two moments are
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp M = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\int_0^{\cos\theta}\\ \amp \qquad (r+1)\,r\,dr\,d\theta,\\ \amp M_y = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\int_0^{\cos\theta}\\ \amp \qquad r\cos\theta\,(r+1)\,r\,dr\,d\theta,\\ \amp M_x = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\!\int_0^{\cos\theta}\\ \amp \qquad r\sin\theta\,(r+1)\,r\,dr\,d\theta, \end{align*}
    and the center of mass is
    \begin{equation*} \bar x = \frac{M_y}{M}, \qquad \bar y = \frac{M_x}{M} . \end{equation*}
    (Since the disk is symmetric about the \(x\)-axis and the density depends on \(r\) alone, the integrand of \(M_x\) is odd in \(\theta\text{:}\) \(M_x = 0\) and \(\bar y = 0\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Consider the integral
    🔗
    \begin{gather*} \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^4\!\!\int_{y/2}^{(y/2)+1}\\ \left(\frac{2x-y}{2} + \frac{z}{3}\right) dx\,dy\,dz . \end{gather*}

    (a)

    Rewrite this integral in such a way that the limits of integration are all constants (as opposed to functions).
    🔗
    Solution.
    The inner limits say \(\frac{y}{2} \le x \le \frac{y}{2} + 1\text{,}\) that is, \(0 \le x - \frac{y}{2} \le 1\text{.}\) So set
    \begin{gather*} u = x - \frac{y}{2},\\ v = y, \qquad w = \frac{z}{3}, \end{gather*}
    which turns the region into the box
    \begin{gather*} 0 \le u \le 1, \quad 0 \le v \le 4,\\ 0 \le w \le 1 . \end{gather*}
    The Jacobian of the map from \((x,y,z)\) to \((u,v,w)\) is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \frac{\partial(u,v,w)}{\partial(x,y,z)}\\ \amp \quad = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & -\frac12 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac13 \end{vmatrix} = \frac13,\\ \amp \text{so}\quad \frac{\partial(x,y,z)}{\partial(u,v,w)} = 3 . \end{align*}
    The integrand is \(\frac{2x-y}{2} + \frac{z}{3} = u + w\text{,}\) and therefore
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_0^3\!\!\int_0^4\!\!\int_{y/2}^{(y/2)+1}\\ \amp \left(\frac{2x-y}{2} + \frac{z}{3}\right) dx\,dy\,dz\\ \amp = \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^4\!\!\int_0^1\\ \amp \quad (u + w)\,(3)\,du\,dv\,dw . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Compute the integral.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The integrand does not involve \(v\text{,}\) so the \(v\)-integral gives a factor of \(4\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^4\!\!\int_0^1\\ \amp \quad 3(u + w)\,du\,dv\,dw\\ \amp = 12\int_0^1\\ \amp \quad \left[\frac{u^2}{2} + uw\right]_{u=0}^{u=1} dw\\ \amp = 12\int_0^1 \left(\frac12 + w\right) dw\\ \amp = 12\left(\frac12 + \frac12\right) = 12 . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    3.

    Use appropriate substitutions to calculate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_R (x + y)\sin(x - y)\,dx\,dy , \end{equation*}
    where \(R\) is the interior of the region enclosed by \(y = x\text{,}\) \(y = x - 2\text{,}\) \(y = -x\text{,}\) and \(y = -x + 1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    The four lines are \(x - y = 0\text{,}\) \(x - y = 2\text{,}\) \(x + y = 0\) and \(x + y = 1\text{,}\) so take
    \begin{equation*} u = x - y, \qquad v = x + y . \end{equation*}
    The region \(R\) becomes the rectangle \(S : 0 \le u \le 2,\ 0 \le v \le 1\) (Figure 4.13.2). Solving for \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} x = \frac{u + v}{2}, \qquad y = \frac{v - u}{2}, \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)} \amp = \begin{vmatrix} \frac12 & \frac12 \\ -\frac12 & \frac12 \end{vmatrix}\\ \amp = \frac14 + \frac14 = \frac12 . \end{align*}
    In the new variables the integrand is \(v\sin u\text{,}\) so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_R (x + y)\sin(x - y)\,dx\,dy\\ \amp = \iint_S v\sin u\\ \amp \qquad \cdot \left|\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\right| du\,dv\\ \amp = \frac12\left(\int_0^2 \sin u\,du\right)\\ \amp \qquad \cdot \left(\int_0^1 v\,dv\right)\\ \amp = \frac12\bigl(1 - \cos 2\bigr)\left(\frac12\right)\\ \amp = \frac14\bigl(1 - \cos 2\bigr) . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    Evaluate the outward flux of the vector field \(\mathbf{F} = x\,\mathbf{i}\) through the closed surface \(S\) of the region bounded by \(z = x^2 + y^2\) and \(z = 1\text{,}\) by computing the flux through each of the two pieces of \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Solution.

    The surface \(S\) consists of the flat disk \(S_1\) on top, where \(z = 1\) and \(x^2 + y^2 \le 1\text{,}\) and the piece \(S_2\) of the paraboloid \(z = x^2 + y^2\) below it, over the same unit disk \(D\) (Figure 4.13.3).
    🔗
    The top \(S_1\text{.}\) The outward normal is \(\hat{\mathbf n} = \mathbf{k}\text{,}\) and \(\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{k} = 0\text{,}\) so the flux through \(S_1\) is \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The paraboloid \(S_2\text{.}\) For the graph \(z = g(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\) the upward normal is \(\hat{\mathbf n}\,d\sigma = \langle -g_x, -g_y, 1\rangle\,dA = \langle -2x, -2y, 1\rangle\,dA\text{.}\) The region lies above \(S_2\text{,}\) so the outward normal there points down:
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \hat{\mathbf n}\,d\sigma = \langle 2x,\ 2y,\ -1\rangle\,dA,\\ \amp \mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,d\sigma\\ \amp = \langle x, 0, 0\rangle\cdot\langle 2x, 2y, -1\rangle\,dA\\ \amp = 2x^2\,dA . \end{align*}
    In polar coordinates over \(D\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_{S_2} \mathbf{F}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,d\sigma\\ \amp = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^1 2r^2\cos^2\theta\; r\,dr\,d\theta\\ \amp = 2\left(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\theta\,d\theta\right)\\ \amp \qquad \cdot \left(\int_0^1 r^3\,dr\right)\\ \amp = 2\,(\pi)\left(\frac14\right) = \frac{\pi}{2} . \end{align*}
    The outward flux through \(S\) is therefore \(0 + \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    5.

    Use Green’s theorem to calculate the line integral
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \oint_C -y\,dx + x\,dy , \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) is the curve \(r = \sin\theta\text{,}\) traversed counterclockwise.
    🔗

    Solution.

    Multiplying \(r = \sin\theta\) by \(r\) gives \(r^2 = r\sin\theta\text{,}\) that is,
    \begin{align*} \amp x^2 + y^2 = y\\ \amp \Longleftrightarrow\ x^2 + \left(y - \tfrac12\right)^2\\ \amp \qquad = \left(\tfrac12\right)^2 , \end{align*}
    the circle of radius \(\frac12\) centered at \(\left(0, \frac12\right)\) (Figure 4.13.4). Let \(R\) be the disk it encloses. With \(M = -y\) and \(N = x\text{,}\) \(\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x} = 1\) and \(\dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} = -1\text{,}\) so by Green’s theorem
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy\\ \amp = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right) dA\\ \amp = \iint_R (1 + 1)\,dA\\ \amp = 2\,\bigl(\text{area of } R\bigr)\\ \amp = 2\,\pi\left(\frac12\right)^2 = \frac{\pi}{2} . \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    6.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \mathbf{F} = yz\,\mathbf{i} - xz\,\mathbf{j} + xy\,e^{z}\,\mathbf{k} , \end{equation*}
    and let \(S\) be the part of the sphere \(x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 25\) with \(z \ge 3\text{,}\) oriented by its upward unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\text{.}\) Use Stokes’ theorem to evaluate
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \iint_S \left(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\right) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma . \end{equation*}

    Solution.

    By Stokes’ theorem (Theorem 3.2.4), the flux of the curl through \(S\) equals the circulation of \(\mathbf{F}\) around the boundary \(C\) of \(S\text{.}\) The cap ends where the sphere meets the plane \(z = 3\text{:}\) there \(x^2 + y^2 = 25 - 9 = 16\text{,}\) so \(C\) is the circle of radius \(4\) centered at \((0,0,3)\) in the plane \(z = 3\text{.}\) The upward normal pairs with the counterclockwise direction when \(C\) is seen from above, so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbf{r}(t) = \left\langle 4\cos t,\ 4\sin t,\ 3 \right\rangle,\\ \amp \quad 0 \le t \le 2\pi . \end{align*}
    Along \(C\text{,}\) \(dz = 0\text{,}\) so the \(\mathbf{k}\)-component of \(\mathbf{F}\) (with its \(e^{z}\)) never enters. With \(x = 4\cos t\text{,}\) \(y = 4\sin t\text{,}\) \(z = 3\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \mathbf{F}\cdot\frac{d\mathbf{r}}{dt}\\ \amp \quad = (3y)(-4\sin t)\\ \amp \qquad {} + (-3x)(4\cos t)\\ \amp \quad = -48\sin^2 t - 48\cos^2 t\\ \amp \quad = -48 , \end{align*}
    and therefore
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_S \left(\nabla \times \mathbf{F}\right) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\, d\sigma\\ \amp \quad = \oint_C \mathbf{F}\cdot d\mathbf{r}\\ \amp \quad = \int_0^{2\pi} (-48)\,dt = -96\pi . \end{align*}
    Check, with a different surface bounded by the same \(C\text{:}\) by (3.2.1),
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \nabla \times \mathbf{F}\\ \amp \quad = x\left(e^{z} + 1\right)\mathbf{i}\\ \amp \qquad {} + y\left(1 - e^{z}\right)\mathbf{j} - 2z\,\mathbf{k} . \end{align*}
    On the flat disk \(x^2 + y^2 \le 16\text{,}\) \(z = 3\text{,}\) with upward normal \(\mathbf{k}\text{,}\) the integrand is the constant \(-2z = -6\text{,}\) and the disk has area \(16\pi\text{,}\) so its flux is \(-6\,(16\pi) = -96\pi\text{,}\) the same. Computing the flux through the curved cap directly would be much harder; Stokes’ theorem lets either the circle or the disk stand in for it.
    🔗

    7.

    True or False? If the statement is true, prove it; if it is false, give a counterexample.
    🔗
    Let \(C\) be a simple closed curve that bounds a region \(R\text{,}\) and let \(M\) and \(N\) have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing \(R\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy\\ \amp = \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right) dA , \end{align*}
    whichever direction \(C\) is traversed in.
    🔗

    Solution.

    False. Green’s theorem holds when \(C\) is traversed counterclockwise, with \(R\) on the left; reversing the direction changes the sign of the line integral but not of the double integral. A counterexample: let \(C\) be the rectangle with vertices \((1,1)\text{,}\) \((3,1)\text{,}\) \((3,4)\) and \((1,4)\text{,}\) traversed clockwise (Figure 4.13.5), with \(M = \ln x + y\) and \(N = -x^2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Here \(\dfrac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\partial M}{\partial y} = -2x - 1 = -(2x + 1)\text{,}\) so the right-hand side is
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \iint_R \left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial M}{\partial y}\right) dA\\ \amp = -\int_1^3\!\!\int_1^4 (2x + 1)\,dy\,dx\\ \amp = -3\left[x^2 + x\right]_{x=1}^{x=3}\\ \amp = -3\,(12 - 2) = -30 . \end{align*}
    By Green’s theorem this is the integral around the rectangle counterclockwise, which we write \(-C\text{.}\) Our \(C\) runs the other way, so
    🔗
    \begin{align*} \amp \oint_{C} M\,dx + N\,dy\\ \amp = -\oint_{-C} M\,dx + N\,dy\\ \amp = -(-30) = 30 \ne -30 . \end{align*}
